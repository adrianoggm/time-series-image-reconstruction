\begin{abstract}
% <= 200 words. Findings in red are filled in from the M10 artefacts.
Image-based representations of time series are widely used as classifier inputs and, increasingly, as the space in which signals are generated or restored, which requires mapping an image back to a series. We present a comparative analysis of how much of a time series each representation retains and can return. We study the most widely used representations, namely the recurrence plot (RP) in its binary, unsigned and signed forms, the Gramian angular summation and difference fields and the Markov transition field, together with the recently proposed extended intertemporal return plot (XIRP), whose diagonal stores the series itself. Each is decoded from its stored 8-bit image alone, with the published inverse where one exists and an operational decoder where none does, and decoding the floating-point matrix separates the loss of the representation from that of storage. On seven datasets from five domains and windows of 64 to 256 samples we measure temporal and spectral fidelity, computational cost, the conditions under which reconstruction is exact, and whether classifiers trained on original windows keep their accuracy on the reconstructions. \pending{key findings}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Turning a time series into an image has become a common way of giving image-domain models access to temporal data. Recurrence plots (RP), Gramian angular fields (GAF) and Markov transition fields (MTF) place a relation between every pair of time instants in a matrix, and convolutional and other image models then learn from that matrix~\cite{wang2015encoding,wang2015encoding2,eckmann1987recurrence,marwan2007recurrence}. The same representations are now also used in the opposite direction: a generative or restoration model works on images, and its output has to be turned back into a series~\cite{huang2023tsdigan,hellermann2026imagegan}.

That second use depends on a property the first never needed. A classifier only has to separate classes, and it can do so from an image that has discarded amplitude, phase or temporal order. A model that produces images is useful only if those images can be decoded into the signal they stand for. Whether, and how well, each representation allows this has not been compared: reconstruction is usually studied for one representation at a time, on one kind of signal, and is often judged only through the accuracy of a downstream classifier.

This paper is a comparative analysis of the reconstruction capacity of image-based time-series representations. We take the most widely used ones, the recurrence plot, the Gramian angular summation and difference fields (GASF, GADF) and the Markov transition field, and add the extended intertemporal return plot (XIRP) of Hellermann and Lessmann~\cite{hellermann2026imagegan}, a recent representation proposed for image-based generation that stores the series itself on its diagonal and is therefore recoverable by construction. For the recurrence plot we separate the encoding from the decoder: we compare the binary RP of the classical literature, the continuous unsigned RP and the signed RP of Lu and Tong~\cite{lu2019robust}, decoded both with the classical reconstruction of Hirata et al.~\cite{hirata2008reproduction} and with a geometric decoder.

The setting is controlled. Every image is the deterministic output of a known encoder, stored with 8 bits per pixel, and every decoder works from the stored image alone. Where a representation has a published inverse we use it; where it has none that can be applied to a stored image, as for the signed RP and GADF, we provide one, since otherwise the comparison could not be made. Decoding the floating-point matrix as well as the 8-bit image separates what the representation loses from what storage loses.

The analysis answers five questions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{RQ1.} How much of the signal can be recovered from each representation, in time and in frequency, and how much of the loss is due to 8-bit storage?
\item \emph{RQ2.} For recurrence plots, what do the encoding and the decoder each contribute?
\item \emph{RQ3.} Under which conditions is reconstruction exact, and how does it vary with the domain, the sampling rate and the window length?
\item \emph{RQ4.} Do reconstructions preserve what classifiers trained on the original signals need?
\item \emph{RQ5.} What does each reconstruction cost in time and memory?
\end{itemize}

The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item A comparative analysis of the reconstruction capacity of five image representations, eight encoding--decoder combinations in total, on seven public datasets from five domains (human activity, environmental audio, electrocardiography, geomagnetism and seismology) and three window lengths, under one protocol of fidelity, cost and classification utility.
\item A separation of the two ingredients of RP reconstruction, encoding and decoder, which shows what the sign of the signed RP adds and what the decoder adds.
\item A statement of what each image determines and of the conditions under which it is exactly, approximately or only partially invertible, with the conditioning of the recurrence-plot states identified as the quantity that governs reconstruction from the signed RP after storage.
\item Operational, metadata-free decoders for the two representations that lacked one, the signed RP and GADF, and the code and derived data needed to reproduce every result.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Image representations of time series}
Recurrence plots were introduced to visualise when a dynamical system revisits a region of its state space~\cite{eckmann1987recurrence} and have since been developed into a family of quantitative tools~\cite{marwan2007recurrence}. In their conventional form they are binary: a pixel marks whether two embedded states lie within a threshold of each other. Continuous variants keep the distance itself, and the signed variant of Lu and Tong also keeps whether the displacement between two states follows a reference direction, which they used for activity recognition from a single accelerometer~\cite{lu2019robust}. Wang and Oates proposed the Gramian angular fields, which map each sample to an angle and store the cosine of their sum or the sine of their difference, and the Markov transition field, which stores transition probabilities between quantile states, as image inputs to convolutional networks~\cite{wang2015encoding,wang2015encoding2}. These representations have been applied to human activity recognition~\cite{ahmad2021inertial}, anomaly detection~\cite{ma2024anomaly} and imputation~\cite{huamin2020reconstruction}. Hellermann and Lessmann recently introduced XIRP for time-series generation with image-based generative adversarial networks; it stores log-returns between instants off the diagonal and the series on the diagonal, so that generated images can be inverted~\cite{hellermann2026imagegan}.

\subsection{Recovering a series from its image}
Inversion has been studied separately for each representation. For binary recurrence plots, Hirata et al.\ build a graph from the recurrence neighbourhoods, approximate the distances between states by shortest paths and recover the state geometry by multidimensional scaling~\cite{hirata2008reproduction}\citetodo{Hirata et al.\ 2016, Sci.\ Rep.\ 6:34982}. For unthresholded recurrence plots of delay embeddings, Sipers et al.\ characterise when the signal is identifiable up to its mean and sign and reconstruct it by optimisation~\cite{sipers2011unique,sipers2017robust}. For GASF, Wang and Oates read the series from the diagonal, and restore damaged images with a denoising autoencoder before doing so~\cite{wang2015encoding}; Tao et al.\ follow a similar route for RP and GAF images~\cite{huamin2020reconstruction}. For GADF no decoder that resolves its branch ambiguity and its lost global offset has been specified, and for MTF even approximate recovery has been reported to be difficult~\cite{wang2015encoding}. XIRP is inverted from its diagonal or by averaging the proposals of all its columns~\cite{hellermann2026imagegan}.

\subsection{Gap}
Each of these works evaluates one representation, usually on one kind of signal, and several evaluate reconstruction only through classification. No study has compared the reconstruction capacity of the representations under a common protocol, separated what a representation loses from what image storage loses, or distinguished, for recurrence plots, the contribution of the encoding from that of the decoder.

\section{Mathematical formulation}\label{sec:formulation}

\subsection{Encoding, storage and decoding}
Let $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_T)$ be one channel of a window. Every method $m$ applies a calibration to a method-specific normalised domain, an encoder $\mathcal E_m$ producing a matrix $M^{(m)}$, a fixed 8-bit storage map $\mathcal Q_m$ and a decoder $\mathcal D_m$:
\[
\hat x = \mathcal N^{-1}\bigl(\mathcal D_m(\mathcal Q_m(\mathcal E_m(\mathcal N(x))));\,x_{\max},x_{\min}\bigr).
\]
The calibration $(x_{\max},x_{\min})$ is shared by all methods and is the only per-channel information used to return to physical units. We call a representation \emph{exactly invertible} when $\mathcal D_m\circ\mathcal E_m$ is the identity on its normalised domain, so that all error in $\hat x$ is due to $\mathcal Q_m$; \emph{approximately invertible} when $\mathcal D_m\circ\mathcal E_m$ is not the identity but recovers the series up to a bounded algorithmic error; and \emph{lossy} when $\mathcal E_m$ is many-to-one.

\subsection{Recurrence plots}
With the adjacent states $s_i=(x_i,x_{i+1})$, $i=1,\ldots,T-1$, and $v=(1,1)/\sqrt2$, the continuous signed RP~\cite{lu2019robust} is
\begin{equation}
R_{ij}=\sigma_{ij}\lVert s_i-s_j\rVert_2,\qquad
\sigma_{ij}=\begin{cases}+1,& \lVert s_i-s_j\rVert=0\ \text{or}\ \hat u_{ij}\cdot v\ge\cos(\pi/4),\\-1,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}
\label{eq:signed_rp}
\end{equation}
with $\hat u_{ij}=(s_i-s_j)/\lVert s_i-s_j\rVert$. The unsigned RP is $|R|$, and the conventional binary RP is $B_{ij}=\mathbb 1[\lVert s_i-s_j\rVert<\varepsilon]$~\cite{eckmann1987recurrence,marwan2007recurrence}. A pair carries directional information only when its displacement lies within $\pi/4$ of $\pm v$: then $\sigma_{ij}=-\sigma_{ji}$, while displacements near the anti-diagonal receive $-1$ in both orders.

\begin{proposition}[Signed RP, floating point]\label{prop:rp}
Let $S\in\mathbb R^{(T-1)\times2}$ be the matrix of states and assume that the centred states span $\mathbb R^2$, i.e.\ $\sigma_2(S-\bar S)>0$. Then classical MDS applied to $|R|$ recovers $S$ up to a translation and an orthogonal transformation $Q\in O(2)$. If, in addition, the adjacent-state overlap $b_i=a_{i+1}$ is satisfied by a unique orientation up to the sign selection of Section~\ref{sec:rp_decoder}, the decoder returns $x$ exactly on the normalised domain.
\end{proposition}
\noindent\emph{Proof sketch.} The first part is the classical result that an exact Euclidean distance matrix of points with affine rank two is reproduced up to isometry by the two leading eigenvectors of the doubly centred squared-distance matrix~\cite{Torgerson1952}. For the second, the overlap residual is linear in $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ for each reflection parity (Section~\ref{sec:rp_decoder}), so the exact orientation attains zero residual. \pending{complete proof and the uniqueness condition, issue \#121}

\begin{remark}[8-bit storage]\label{rem:conditioning}
Storage perturbs $|R|$ by at most half a quantisation step relative to its range. The error of the recovered two-dimensional subspace grows as the gap between the second and third eigenvalues of the centred Gram matrix shrinks, so the reconstruction degrades as $\sigma_2/\sigma_1$ of the centred states tends to zero, i.e.\ as the states become collinear\citetodo{eigenvector perturbation bound, e.g.\ Davis--Kahan}. Empirically, no series with $\sigma_2/\sigma_1\ge0.1$ exceeds a normalised error of $0.01$ (Section~\ref{sec:results_conditions}).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Gramian angular fields}
After scaling to the method-specific interval and $\phi_i=\arccos\tilde x_i$, the summation and difference fields are $G^{\mathrm S}_{ij}=\cos(\phi_i+\phi_j)$ and $G^{\mathrm D}_{ij}=\sin(\phi_i-\phi_j)$~\cite{wang2015encoding}. On $[0,1]$, $G^{\mathrm S}_{ii}=\cos(2\phi_i)$ determines $\tilde x_i$ uniquely, so GASF is exactly invertible. GADF has $G^{\mathrm D}_{ii}=0$; on the symmetric interval $[-\alpha,\alpha]$ the inverse sine returns $\phi_i-\phi_j$ without branch ambiguity if and only if $\alpha<1/\sqrt2$, and the remaining global offset is fixed by an image-derived boundary anchor (Section~\ref{sec:gadf_decoder}). Under these hypotheses GADF is exactly invertible.

\subsection{Markov transition fields and XIRP}
MTF assigns every sample to one of $Q$ quantile states and stores the transition probability between the states occupied at two positions~\cite{wang2015encoding}. Samples in the same state are indistinguishable, so the operator is many-to-one and only a representative trajectory can be recovered. XIRP~\cite{hellermann2026imagegan} requires a strictly positive series and stores $R_{ii}=x_i$ and $R_{ij}=\log(x_i/x_j)$; it is exactly invertible from its diagonal, and inversion by mean averages the $T$ column proposals $x_j\exp(R_{ij})$.

\subsection{Storage protocol}
Each matrix is written to $[0,255]$ by a fixed or per-window affine map. GASF and GADF are read back with the fixed operator range $[-1,1]$, MTF with $[0,1]$, and XIRP with the fixed ranges of its diagonal and off-diagonal parts after the series is mapped to $[1,2]$. The signed RP keeps a per-window map; its zero-valued diagonal locates $R=0$, so subtracting the median diagonal pixel recovers the signed matrix up to a positive scale that MDS and the final range map absorb. No decoder uses per-window metadata.

\subsection{Signed-RP decoder}\label{sec:rp_decoder}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Recover $R^{\mathrm{obs}}=\mathrm{PNG}-\operatorname{median}_i\mathrm{PNG}_{ii}$ and the symmetric distances $D_{ij}=\tfrac12(|R^{\mathrm{obs}}_{ij}|+|R^{\mathrm{obs}}_{ji}|)$.
\item Classical MDS~\cite{Torgerson1952}: with $J=I-\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top/(T-1)$, the two leading eigenpairs of $-\tfrac12JD^{\circ2}J$ give $Y=V_2\Lambda_2^{1/2}$.
\item Orientation in closed form. Every $Q\in O(2)$ is $\mathrm{rot}(\theta)\operatorname{diag}(1,\eta)$ with $\eta\in\{\pm1\}$. Writing $Z=YQ$ and $(a_i,b_i)=(Z_{i1},Z_{i2})$, the overlap $b_i=a_{i+1}$ must hold up to a common translation $\delta$. For fixed $\eta$ the residual $b_i-a_{i+1}=A_i\cos\theta+B_i\sin\theta$ with $A_i=\eta Y_{i2}-Y_{i+1,1}$ and $B_i=-\eta Y_{i1}-Y_{i+1,2}$; profiling out $\delta$ centres $A$ and $B$, and the objective becomes the Rayleigh quotient $u^\top H_\eta u$ with $H_\eta=\tilde C^\top\tilde C$, $\tilde C=[\tilde A\ \tilde B]$ and $u=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$. Its minimiser is the eigenvector of the smaller eigenvalue of the $2\times2$ matrix $H_\eta$, so the two parities give four exact candidates.
\item Sign selection. Among the four candidates, keep the one whose regenerated signed RP is closest to $R^{\mathrm{obs}}$ in Frobenius norm after both are normalised to unit norm. The orientation is chosen from the image alone; the original series is never consulted.
\item Samples: $\hat x_1=a_1$, $\hat x_i=\tfrac12(b_{i-1}+a_i)$ for $2\le i\le T-1$, $\hat x_T=b_{T-1}$, and the minimum and maximum are mapped to the normalised range.
\end{enumerate}
The orientation costs $O(T)$ and the decoder is dominated by the eigendecomposition, $O(T^3)$ in a dense implementation.
On the unsigned RP the same decoder runs without step~4, so the reflection remains undetermined.

\subsection{Classical reconstruction of Hirata et al.}\label{sec:hirata}
As the reference for the conventional RP we implement the reconstruction of Hirata et al.~\cite{hirata2008reproduction}\citetodo{Hirata et al.\ 2016}: from the binary RP, an edge joins $i\ne j$ when $B_{ij}=1$, with local length $1-|G_i\cap G_j|/|G_i\cup G_j|$, $G_i=\{k:B_{ik}=1\}$; all-pairs shortest paths give a distance matrix, and classical MDS gives the states. The threshold $\varepsilon$ is not prescribed by the method; we set it to a recurrence rate of $0.75$. Turning the states into $T$ samples is our post-processing: the leading MDS coordinate, the mean appended to reach $T$ samples, and the range map. A binary RP carries no sign, so the reflection is left as returned. When Hirata's method is applied to the signed RP, its graph sees only the thresholded magnitude, so the sign is used, as in our decoder, only to choose between the series and its reflection.

\subsection{GASF, GADF, MTF and XIRP decoders}\label{sec:gadf_decoder}
GASF uses the diagonal inverse $\hat{\tilde x}_i=\cos(\tfrac12\arccos G^{\mathrm S}_{ii})$~\cite{wang2015encoding}. For GADF on $[-\alpha,\alpha]$ with $\alpha=0.7$, the row means $\mu_i=\frac1T\sum_jG^{\mathrm D}_{ij}$ are strictly increasing in $\phi_i$, so $e=\arg\max_i|\mu_i|$ identifies a boundary sample and the sign of $\mu_e$ fixes which boundary; the angles follow from $\hat\phi^{(p)}_j=\phi_e-\arcsin G^{\mathrm D}_{ep}-\arcsin G^{\mathrm D}_{pj}$, averaged over the five pivots with the smallest $|\mu_p|$. MTF ($Q=4$) is decoded to a representative trajectory by clustering the rows into $Q$ states, ordering them by the Fiedler vector of the symmetrised transition matrix~\cite{Atkins1998} and assigning each state its bin midpoint. XIRP uses inversion by mean~\cite{hellermann2026imagegan}.

\subsection{Windows and splits}
Every channel is encoded, stored and decoded independently, as its own single-channel 8-bit image with its own calibration, whatever the number of channels: one image for ESC-50, three for WISDM, Swarm and STEAD, and eight, nine and twelve for EMG, PAMAP2 and PTB-XL. Channels are neither packed into colour planes nor combined, so results are comparable across datasets and cost grows linearly with the number of channels; representations that couple channels are outside the scope of this study. Each recording is cut into non-overlapping windows of $T\in\{64,128,256\}$ samples that never cross a label change or a time gap. For fidelity, 500 windows per dataset and length are drawn with a fixed seed. For classification, up to 6\,000 windows per dataset and length are drawn, and three grouped folds keep every subject, patient, event, station or source recording on one side; folds are stratified by class for single-label data. Blocks with fewer than 200 windows (EMG at $T=256$, where a gesture lasts about three seconds) are not reported. ESC-50 at 128 samples spans 16\,ms of sound; audio is therefore also evaluated on the official ESC-10 subset at 2, 4 and 8\,kHz with windows of 512 and 1\,024 samples, silent windows removed and accuracy also scored per clip.

\subsection{Fidelity, cost and utility}
Fidelity is measured per channel series with range-normalised errors, writing $r=\max_ix_i-\min_ix_i$:
\[
\mathrm{NMAE}=\frac{1}{rT}\sum_i|x_i-\hat x_i|,\qquad
\mathrm{NRMSE}=\frac1r\sqrt{\tfrac1T\sum_i(x_i-\hat x_i)^2},
\]
together with Pearson correlation, the range-normalised error of first differences, the $L_1$ distance between unit-mass Welch spectra~\cite{Welch1967}, the dominant-frequency error and the energy error. Constant series are counted and excluded. Each decoder is run on the floating-point operator and on the 8-bit image, which separates algorithmic from storage error. Cost is the single-thread time to encode and decode and the peak traced memory, measured for $T$ from 64 to 1\,024.

Utility follows the train-on-original protocol: KNN, SVM, random forest, LSTM, GRU and a one-dimensional CNN are trained on original windows and evaluated on the original and on every reconstruction of the same held-out windows.

\subsection{Statistical protocol}
All comparisons are paired over the same series. Differences between decoders are summarised by medians with $95\%$ bootstrap intervals that resample whole groups~\cite{DavisonHinkley1997,Hall1987}, tested with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{Wilcoxon1945} and Holm's correction within each family~\cite{Holm1979}, and averaged across datasets with equal weight. Classification equivalence requires the $95\%$ group-bootstrap interval of the accuracy difference to lie within $\pm0.01$~\cite{Lakens2017}.
