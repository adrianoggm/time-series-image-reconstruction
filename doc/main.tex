\begin{abstract}
Image-based encodings of time series -- Recurrence Plots (RP), Markov Transition Fields (MTF), and the summation (GASF) and difference (GADF) variants of Gramian Angular Fields -- make temporal structure available to image-domain models \cite{wang2015encoding,wang2015encoding2}. This work does not evaluate generative augmentation directly. Instead, it studies the prerequisite inverse problem: whether images produced by known encoders can be mapped back to triaxial accelerometer windows with controlled loss.

We contribute three operational decoders that, to the best of our knowledge, have no prior fully specified counterpart in the literature: (i) a signed-RP decoder that uses the Lu--Tong sign convention as an in-loop constraint to resolve the multidimensional-scaling reflection ambiguity left open by Hirata, Horai, and Aihara \cite{hirata2008reproduction}, together with explicit handling of the $N\to N-1$ adjacent-state embedding; (ii) a closed-form GADF pivot-row decoder that turns the previously stated but under-specified invertibility of GADF on restricted angular ranges into an explicit reconstruction algorithm, with branch control, angular anchoring, sign convention, and scale calibration; and (iii) a Fiedler-vector spectral MTF decoder that reconstructs a representative quantile trajectory compatible with the stored MTF image. A practical GASF inversion building on the prior diagonal-inverse line of work \cite{huamin2020reconstruction} closes the four-encoding comparison as a tightly controlled baseline.

The four decoders are evaluated under a unified four-lens protocol -- visual inspection, pointwise error, polynomial-trend similarity, and a six-classifier original-trained robustness evaluation -- on WISDM windows of length $N=128$, under both LOTO and grouped subject-out 3-fold cross-validation. GASF is near-original on every lens, shows no statistically significant classification degradation relative to the raw signal under paired McNemar tests, and has observed accuracy differences bounded by $0.001$ in this protocol. We therefore treat it as empirically near-equivalent for the evaluated WISDM task, without claiming formal information preservation beyond this controlled setting. The restricted-branch GADF decoder is consistently second and, under subject-out evaluation, is the only reconstruction that strictly beats the original on one classifier (KNN). The bottom of the ranking is lens-dependent: RP wins on the pointwise lens but MTF wins on the classifier lens on five of the six classifiers, a clean rank inversion that the unified protocol makes explicit. Beyond the ranking itself, we show that the visual proximity between RP and GADF is structural rather than coincidental: GADF lives in the antisymmetric pairwise angular-difference matrix space by construction, while signed RP only approximates a related directional pairwise-distance structure through an external orientation rule. Within the controlled reconstruction protocol studied here, GADF dominates RP on every measured metric; we therefore recommend the pair $\{\text{GASF},\text{GADF}\}$ as the canonical reconstruction-oriented GAF baseline and reserve RP for use cases where recurrence geometry or dynamical-systems diagnostics are themselves the object of study. These results show that, when the inverse map and the encoder-side metadata are explicitly controlled, image-based encodings can support a near-original reconstruction -- a necessary first step toward, but not yet a proof of, unconstrained generative augmentation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Time series data play a crucial role in fields ranging from finance and healthcare to human activity recognition. Despite their importance, many applications still struggle with the lack of robust and versatile techniques for data augmentation. This gap has motivated researchers to explore alternative representations that can leverage advanced methods from adjacent domains most notably, the rich domain of image processing.

A common approach is to convert time-series signals into image-based representations. Techniques such as Markov Transition Fields (MTF), Recurrence Plots (RP), and Gramian Angular Fields (GAF) transform a one-dimensional sequence into a two-dimensional matrix whose pixel \((i,j)\) encodes a relation between two time indices. For triaxial accelerometer windows, the three axes are encoded independently and then treated as image channels. This construction allows image analysis and generation models to operate on temporal structure that is often difficult to expose directly in the raw signal.

Parallel to these developments, diffusion models have gained prominence in image generation. Their ability to synthesize highly structured images motivates the use of image representations for time-series augmentation, but this paper does not train or evaluate a diffusion or GAN model. We focus on the necessary inverse problem that must be solved first: if an image is produced by a known time-series encoder, can it be reconstructed back into the signal domain with controlled loss?

Our approach addresses this inverse problem by proposing dedicated reconstruction strategies for RP, MTF, and GAF-based encodings, and by evaluating the completed decoders under a unified framework that includes visual inspection, reconstruction metrics, polynomial trend comparison, and downstream classification. The overall workflow is summarized in Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow_overview}. Experiments on the WISDM Smartphone and Smartwatch Activity and Biometrics Dataset \cite{misc_wisdm_smartphone_and_smartwatch_activity_and_biometrics_dataset__507} show that the evaluated encodings preserve different aspects of the original signals and therefore support different use cases.

A central narrative choice in this paper is to separate \emph{controlled reconstruction} from \emph{unconstrained generative augmentation}. The experiments decode images produced by known encoders and, when required, use metadata stored during encoding, such as min--max ranges. This setting is the necessary first test of whether an image representation contains enough information to return to the time domain. It is not yet a claim that arbitrary images produced by a generative model will be physically plausible or reconstructable without additional calibration.


The paper is organized around three questions: what information each image encoding preserves, how that information can be mapped back to the time domain, and whether the reconstructed signals remain useful for downstream activity recognition. The main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a common operator-level formulation \(M_{ij}=\omega(\xi(x_i),\xi(x_j))\) that, in the conclusion, lets us read signed RP and GADF as structurally adjacent parametrisations of directional pairwise differences --- GADF lying in the antisymmetric zero-diagonal matrix space by construction, signed RP approximating it without exact antisymmetry under the Lu--Tong sign rule;
    \item \textbf{a novel RP decoder} that is the first to exploit the signed Lu--Tong RP definition to resolve the orientation ambiguity left open by the Hirata--Horai--Aihara reconstruction \cite{hirata2008reproduction}, together with an explicit treatment of the $N{-}1$ adjacent-state shrinkage and a quantisation-aware post-processing step;
    \item \textbf{a novel restricted-branch GADF pivot-row decoder} that operationalises the earlier observation by Wang and Oates \cite{wang2015encoding} that GADF can admit an accurate inverse map under restricted scaling: the contribution is a fully specified closed-form decoder with an explicit $\arcsin$ branch condition, image-derived angular anchor, fixed sign convention, external min--max calibration, and empirical evaluation under the same protocol as RP, MTF, and GASF, so it should not be read as a generic inverse for unconstrained GADF images;
    \item \textbf{a novel Fiedler-vector MTF decoder}, for which we are not aware of a prior explicit MTF-to-time-series decoder: rows of the field are clustered into $Q$ bins, the bin transition matrix is recovered, and the bin ordering is fixed by the Fiedler vector of the corresponding graph Laplacian; because MTF is quantized and many-to-one, the output is a representative compatible quantile trajectory rather than a bijective inverse;
    \item a GASF inversion pipeline that extends the diagonal-inverse line of work \cite{huamin2020reconstruction} with explicit handling of normalisation and quantisation, kept in the comparison as a controlled prior-art baseline rather than as a novel decoder;
    \item an explicit account of the assumptions that make each inverse map exact, approximate, or heuristic, and the metadata that each one requires;
    \item an evaluation design that separates pointwise fidelity, trend-level similarity, and classification utility, so that good classification is not mistaken for accurate signal reconstruction;
    \item a fully comparable evaluation of all four encodings (RP, MTF, GASF, GADF) under the same protocol -- same fold, same six-classifier panel, same pointwise and polynomial-trend metrics, both LOTO and grouped subject-out 3-fold.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work and Encodings}
\label{sec:related_encodings}
This section fixes the notation and summarizes the encoding mechanisms needed for the inverse problem. We distinguish between the image construction rule, which is part of the prior literature, and the reconstruction rule, which is where this paper contributes or identifies an explicit gap. For clarity, GAF denotes the family, whereas GASF and GADF denote the summation and difference variants, respectively.

All four encodings can be written as operators \(\mathcal{T}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}\), but they preserve different kinds of information. RP encodes geometric recurrences in a phase-space embedding, GASF and GADF encode angular relations after polar normalization, and MTF encodes transition probabilities between quantized states. This distinction is important for the rest of the paper: a representation that is useful for classification is not necessarily pointwise invertible, and a representation that is highly invertible under controlled conditions may still require metadata that are difficult to obtain for generated images.

\subsection{Recurrence Plot}
\subsubsection{Image Construction}
Recurrence plots were introduced by Eckmann et al. \cite{eckmann1995recurrence} and are commonly interpreted through phase-space reconstruction ideas developed for nonlinear dynamical systems \cite{takens1981detecting,marwan2007recurrence}. A scalar time series can be embedded as delay vectors
\[
\mathbf{s}_t=(x_t,x_{t+\tau},\ldots,x_{t+(m-1)\tau})\in\mathbb{R}^m,
\]
where \(m\) is the embedding dimension and \(\tau\) is the delay. In this work, we use the first-order embedding \(m=2\), \(\tau=1\), so \(s_i=(x_i,x_{i+1})\). The classical recurrence matrix is then defined as
\begin{equation}
   \mathbf{R}_{i,j}(\epsilon) = \boldsymbol{\theta}(\epsilon - \|s_i - s_j\|), \quad i,j = 1,\ldots,N-1,
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon$ is a threshold that determines the neighborhood size, $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ is the Heaviside step function, and $\|\cdot\|$ denotes a norm. Choosing an appropriate norm is crucial for constructing a meaningful recurrence plot; the most commonly used norms are $L_1$, $L_2$, and $L_\infty$ \cite{lu2019robust,he2016deep}.

One limitation of this definition is that the distance from \(x_i\) to \(x_j\) equals that from \(x_j\) to \(x_i\), yielding a symmetric matrix with respect to the main diagonal. Consequently, the mapping is not bijective: a time series and its inverted version produce identical images, meaning that decoding the image would yield two possible time-series representations. To overcome this issue, we adopt the alternative definition proposed by Jianjie Lu and Kai‐Yu Tong \cite{lu2019robust}, which addresses the bijective image representation problem and is formulated as follows:

Consider a time series \( x \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times N} \), where \( x_j \) denotes the \( j \)-th element of \( x \). The states are then defined as
\[
s_j = \bigl(x_j,\, x_{j+1}\bigr), \quad \text{with } s_j \in \mathbb{R}^2,
\]
so that the recurrence plot (RP) is constructed as a matrix \( R \in \mathbb{R}^{(N-1) \times (N-1)} \).

A basic formulation of the RP is given by
\begin{equation}
   R_{m,n} = \| s_m - s_n \|, \quad m,n = 1,\ldots,N-1,
\end{equation}
where \(\|\cdot\|\) denotes a chosen norm (e.g., \(L_1\), \(L_2\), or \(L_\infty\)).

To incorporate directional information, we calculate a sign based on the differences between state vectors. Throughout this work we use the fixed reference direction \(v=(1,1)^{\top}/\sqrt{2}\). For off-diagonal entries, define
\[
\gamma_{mn}=\frac{(s_m-s_n)\cdot v}{\|s_m-s_n\|\,\|v\|}, \qquad m\ne n.
\]
The sign convention assigns a negative value only when the off-diagonal displacement vector forms an angle greater than \(3\pi/4\) with respect to \(v\):
\begin{equation}
   \text{sign}(m,n)=
\begin{cases}
  1,  & \text{if } m=n, \\[0.5ex]
  -1, & \text{if } m\ne n \text{ and } \gamma_{mn}<\cos\left(\dfrac{3\pi}{4}\right), \\[1ex]
  1,  & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where \(\|v\|=1\). The diagonal convention avoids the undefined ratio at \(m=n\); since \(R_{m,m}=0\) after multiplication by the distance, the chosen diagonal sign has no numerical effect. This convention is used consistently during both RP encoding and the reconstruction-orientation check.

Finally, the recurrence plot is redefined as
\begin{equation}
   R_{m,n} = \text{sign}(m,n) \cdot \| s_m - s_n \|,
   \label{eq:FormulaRP}
\end{equation}
which is the definition we use to encode the RP as an image after normalizing the values and mapping them to RGB.

The adjacent-state construction produces \(N-1\) embedded states rather than \(N\) scalar samples. The information loss is not merely a count mismatch, since the overlapping states still contain all samples in principle; it arises because the RP stores pairwise distances or thresholded recurrences, which are invariant to transformations such as translation, rotation, reflection, and, depending on normalization, scale. The signed Lu--Tong convention used here adds an orientation cue, but it does not make the RP representation fully bijective.

\subsubsection{Time Series Reconstruction}
There is no direct inverse transformation for converting time series into recurrence plots according to the literature. However, several approaches have been proposed over time, primarily because this is one of the oldest methods for reconstruction. Notable contributions include those by M. Thiel \cite{THIEL2004343} and Tanio et al. \cite{TANIO20092031}. In particular, we highlight the decoding method by Hirata,. \cite{hirata2008reproduction}, which, as shown later, is adapted here to our signed RP definition (see Equation \ref{eq:FormulaRP}). This method is applicable to both continuous and binary recurrence plots.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Starting from a Recurrence Plot:}
    Let \(z(i)\) denote the state at time \(i\) (with \(1 \leq i \leq n\)). Define a function \(c\) that takes two states and indicates whether they are sufficiently close; it returns 1 if they are close and 0 otherwise, according to a predefined threshold. Thus, the recurrence plot can be represented as a binary cost matrix:
    \[
    \{(i,j) : c(z_i,z_j)=1,\quad i,j = 1,2,\dots,n\}.
    \]

    \item \textbf{Graph Construction:}
    Construct a graph in which an edge is drawn between nodes \(i\) and \(j\) if \(c(z_i,z_j)=1\).

    \item \textbf{Edge Weight Assignment:}
    For each state \(z_i\), define the set
    \[
    G_i = \{j : c(z_i,z_j)=1\},
    \]
    which represents the indices of time where state \(z_i\) is close to other states. Then, assign a weight to each edge between \(i\) and \(j\) as follows:
    \[
    w(i,j)= 1-\frac{|G_i \cap G_j|}{|G_i \cup G_j|}.
    \]

    \item \textbf{Shortest Path Calculation:}
    Compute the shortest path distances in the graph using Dijkstra's algorithm \cite{cherkassky1996shortest}. For larger graphs, an alternative method for finding the shortest path can be employed.

    \item \textbf{Multidimensional Scaling:}
    Finally, apply a multidimensional scaling technique that orders the set of points using the component corresponding to the largest eigenvalue. We use \emph{classical} (Torgerson) MDS, i.e.\ the closed-form double-centering eigendecomposition of $-\tfrac{1}{2}J D^{2} J$ with $J=I-\tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}$, rather than the SMACOF iterative optimiser. For a Euclidean-compatible distance matrix, this is the exact least-squares embedding, rather than an iterative approximation, and it removes the stochastic dependence on initialization that an iterative SMACOF solver would introduce.
\end{enumerate}

Although this graph-based method can recover a consistent state ordering, it has a notable drawback, also discussed in \cite{hirata2008reproduction}: multidimensional scaling may return a mirror-symmetric reconstruction. In our WISDM experiments, this ambiguity was observed before applying the signed-RP orientation check described below; therefore, the observed ambiguity should be understood as an empirical diagnostic of this implementation rather than as a parameter from the cited method.
\subsection{Gramian Angular Field}
\subsubsection{Image Construction}
\label{ConstruccionGAF}
Introduced by Wang, Z. and Oates, T. in \textit{Imaging Time-Series to Improve Classification and Imputation} \cite{wang2015encoding}, the Gramian Angular Field (GAF) technique represents time series values in polar coordinates. To do so, it is essential to rescale the time series values to an appropriate interval either to \([-1,1]\) to map to angles in \([0,\pi]\), or to \([0,1]\) to map to angles in \([0,\pi/2]\).

Once the time series is rescaled, the encoding is defined using the arccosine and the radius. Specifically, for the interval \([0,\pi]\) the definitions are:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{cases}
    \phi = \arccos(\tilde{x}_i), \quad -1 \leq \tilde{x}_i \leq 1,\quad \tilde{x}_i \in \tilde{X}, \\
    r = \frac{t_i}{N}, \quad t_i \in \mathbb{N},
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
and for the interval \([0,\pi/2]\) they become:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{cases}
    \phi = \arccos(\tilde{x}_i), \quad 0 \leq \tilde{x}_i \leq 1,\quad \tilde{x}_i \in \tilde{X}, \\
    r = \frac{t_i}{N}, \quad t_i \in \mathbb{N}.
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}

According to Wang and Oates \cite{wang2015encoding}, the polar transformation preserves temporal ordering through the radius and maps normalized amplitudes to angles through a monotonic arccosine branch. This does not mean that every GAF matrix has the same inverse properties. GASF and GADF use different pairwise angular operators, so the reconstruction argument must be derived separately for each case: GASF exposes amplitude information on the diagonal, whereas GADF stores only relative angular differences and has a zero diagonal.

Finally, two approaches to obtain a GAF are defined:
\begin{align}
G^{\mathrm{S}}_{ij} &= \cos(\phi_i + \phi_j) \nonumber \\
&= \tilde{x}_i\tilde{x}_j - \sqrt{1-\tilde{x}_i^2}\sqrt{1-\tilde{x}_j^2}, \label{eq:gasf_indexed}\\
G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ij} &= \sin(\phi_i - \phi_j) \nonumber \\
&= \sqrt{1-\tilde{x}_i^2}\tilde{x}_j - \tilde{x}_i\sqrt{1-\tilde{x}_j^2}. \label{eq:gadf_indexed}
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Time Series Reconstruction}
\label{ReconstruccionGAF}
The two GAF variants require different reconstruction arguments. GASF has a known diagonal-based inverse under the appropriate normalization range, whereas GADF cannot be treated by simply reusing the same diagonal argument. We therefore separate the current GASF baseline from the GADF off-diagonal reconstruction developed below.

\paragraph{GASF reconstruction.}
In the original GAF paper \cite{wang2015encoding}, the possibility of recovering the original time series is connected to the structure of the Gramian representation. In subsequent literature, including a later paper by the same authors \cite{wang2015encoding2}, the practical reconstruction route is discussed through the Gramian Angular Summation Field (GASF) main diagonal.

For GASF inversion, we use the diagonal argument discussed by Huamin, Tao, and Qiuqun in \textit{Reconstruction of Time Series with Missing Values Using a 2D Representation Based Denoising Autoencoder} \cite{huamin2020reconstruction}, because it states the normalization conditions under which the diagonal can be used for reconstruction. Under those conditions, the normalized time series can be reconstructed by observing the main diagonal of a GASF. Specifically, since the diagonal elements satisfy
\[
G^{\mathrm{S}}_{ii} = \cos(2\phi_i),
\]
one can recover the normalized sample using the half-angle identity:
\[
\tilde{x}_i = \cos \phi_i = \sqrt{\frac{G^{\mathrm{S}}_{ii} + 1}{2}}, \quad \phi_i \in \left[0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right].
\]

It is important to note that the main-diagonal inversion is not unique for the interval \([-1,1]\), which maps to angles in \([0,\pi]\). In that case,
\[
G^{\mathrm{S}}_{ii}=\cos(2\phi_i)=2\tilde{x}_i^2-1,
\]
so the half-angle identity recovers only \(|\tilde{x}_i|\), not the sign of \(\tilde{x}_i\). This limitation applies to the diagonal-only inversion rule used here; it does not constitute a proof that no reconstruction could be obtained from the full GASF matrix or from additional side information.

Wang and Oates state that the GADF of $[0,1]$-rescaled data has an accurate inverse map \cite{wang2015encoding}. However, their reconstruction discussion develops the practical inverse through the GASF diagonal and does not provide an explicit GADF decoding algorithm. In particular, a GADF decoder must resolve the branch of $\arcsin$, fix the otherwise free global angular offset implied by the difference-only form $\sin(\phi_i-\phi_j)$, keep the sign convention fixed, and map the recovered angular trajectory back to physical units. Reconstruction via the main diagonal is not feasible since the angular differences yield
\[
G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ii} = \sin(\phi_i - \phi_i) = 0,
\]
regardless of whether the time series is scaled to \([-1,1]\) with angles in \([0,\pi]\) or to \([0,1]\) with angles in \([0,\pi/2]\). The method proposed later in Section~\ref{sec:gadf_reconstruction_proposal} therefore uses off-diagonal angular differences rather than the diagonal.


\subsection{Markov Transition Field}
\label{sec:mtf_definition}
\subsubsection{Image Construction}
Introduced by Wang and Oates in \textit{Imaging Time-Series to Improve Classification and Imputation} \cite{wang2015encoding}, the Markov Transition Field (MTF) is probabilistic rather than geometric or trigonometric. Given a time series \(X\), the values are divided into \(Q\) quantile bins and each sample is assigned a discrete state \(q_t\in\{1,\ldots,Q\}\). The first-order Markov transition matrix is estimated as
\[
W_{p,q}=P(q_{t+1}=q\mid q_t=p)=\frac{\#\{t:q_t=p,\ q_{t+1}=q\}}{\#\{t:q_t=p\}},
\]
with rows normalized to sum to one when the source state is observed.

The MTF expands this \(Q\times Q\) matrix back to an \(N\times N\) image by assigning each pixel the transition probability associated with the states of its two time indices:
\begin{equation}
M_{a,b}=W_{q_a,q_b},\qquad a,b=1,\ldots,N.
\end{equation}
Thus, each pixel stores a transition statistic between quantile states rather than a direct distance or angular relation between amplitudes. This explains why MTF can be robust for classification while being weak for pointwise reconstruction: all samples inside the same quantile share the same reconstructed representative value.

\subsubsection{Time Series Reconstruction }
In the literature, there is no documented method for estimating the original time series from a Markov Transition Field (MTF) matrix. Some articles use Markov Random Fields for image estimation and reconstruction, applying them as image filters to improve reconstructed image quality \cite{kopec1994document,kliewer2006iterative}. However, those methods address image restoration rather than inverse MTF decoding.




\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem_formulation}
Let \(x=(x_1,\ldots,x_N)\) denote one axis of a triaxial accelerometer window, with \(N=128\) in all experiments. For an encoding method \(m\), the image construction is written as
\[
\mathcal{E}_m:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N_m\times N_m\times C},
\]
where \(C\) denotes the number of image channels after the matrix representation is normalized and stored. In our setting, \(N_m=N\) for GASF, GADF, and MTF, while the modified RP representation internally uses adjacent states and therefore has \(N_m=N-1\) before the reconstruction is resampled back to \(N\). The inverse problem studied in this paper is to construct a decoder
\[
\mathcal{D}_m:\mathbb{R}^{N_m\times N_m\times C}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
\]
such that \(\hat{x}=\mathcal{D}_m(\mathcal{E}_m(x))\) is close to the original signal under pointwise, trend-level, and downstream classification criteria. The same notation is used for each accelerometer axis; the triaxial reconstruction is obtained by applying the decoder independently to the three encoded channels and then regrouping the axes into a single window.

This formulation separates three sources of difficulty. First, some encodings introduce invariances or many-to-one summaries by construction. In RP, the adjacent-state embedding produces \(N-1\) overlapping two-dimensional states that still contain all samples in principle, but the stored pairwise distances or recurrences are invariant to transformations such as translation, rotation, reflection, and, depending on normalization, scale; the decoder then also has to map the recovered \(N-1\) state sequence back to \(N\) samples. Second, some encodings introduce branch or orientation ambiguities, as in GASF diagonal inversion, GADF sine-difference inversion, and multidimensional scaling for RP. Third, image storage and normalization introduce practical quantization and rescaling errors. The proposed decoders therefore report their assumptions explicitly: normalization range, required metadata, orientation convention, and whether the reconstruction is exact, approximate, or heuristic.

The rest of the paper follows this formulation. Section~\ref{sec:proposed_methods} describes the decoders, including the proposed GADF pivot-row reconstruction. Section~\ref{sec:experimental_protocol} defines the data and metrics, Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the completed empirical evidence for RP, MTF, and GASF together with the GADF result slots, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets the trade-offs among encodings. This organization is intentional: methods may be mathematically defined before all empirical evidence is available, but comparative claims are restricted to the decoders tested under the same protocol.

\section{Proposed Reconstruction Methods}
\label{sec:proposed_methods}
This section describes the reconstruction strategy associated with each encoding. Each method is presented as an inverse map with explicit assumptions, so that later experiments can be read as tests of these assumptions rather than as isolated implementation results.
\subsection{Recurrence Plot}
\label{sec:rp_reconstruction_method}
The Recurrence Plot decoder proposed below is a \emph{new} contribution of this work; the closest precedent in the literature is the unsigned reconstruction of Hirata, Horai, and Aihara \cite{hirata2008reproduction}, which terminates at the multidimensional-scaling step and leaves both the orientation ambiguity (a global sign flip after MDS) and the $N\rightarrow N-1$ shrinkage of the adjacent-state embedding unresolved. We extend that pipeline in two ways that, taken together, turn it into a usable inverse map for the signed RP variant studied in this paper: we exploit the \emph{signed} Lu--Tong RP definition (Equation~\ref{eq:FormulaRP}) to disambiguate the MDS reflection by an empirical orientation check, and we re-augment the reconstructed sequence from $N-1$ adjacent-state points back to $N$ samples through a per-window mean appended before cubic-spline resampling. The signed-RP orientation check is, to the best of our knowledge, the first use of the signed RP convention as a constraint inside a reconstruction decoder rather than as an encoder-side choice. The decoder is therefore presented as a controlled inverse map: the encoder--decoder pair is well defined on the protocol of this paper, but neither step is universally invertible.

Multidimensional scaling can recover a compatible one-dimensional geometry, but only up to translation, scale, and reflection; therefore, the decoder must explicitly restore the missing sample and select the orientation most consistent with the signed RP convention. Concretely:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Step 1:} Define a recurrence-rate parameter \(\rho=0.75\). Let \(d_{ij}=\|z_i-z_j\|_2\) be the Euclidean distance between states and let \(\tau\) be the empirical \(\rho\)-quantile of the off-diagonal distances. We set \(c_{ij}=1\) if \(d_{ij}\leq\tau\), and \(c_{ij}=0\) otherwise. Thus, the 75\% closest state pairs are considered recurrent.
    \item \textbf{Step 2:} Construct a graph from the binary matrix by establishing an edge between nodes \(i\) and \(j\) if \(c_{ij} = 1\); no edge is created if \(c_{ij} = 0\).
    \item \textbf{Step 3:} Assign a weight to each edge. Define
    \[
    G_i = \{ j \mid c(z_i, z_j) = 1 \},
    \]
    which represents the set of time indices where state \(z_i\) is close to other states. Then, for each edge between \(i\) and \(j\), set the weight as
    \[
    w(i, j) = 1 - \frac{|G_i \cap G_j|}{|G_i \cup G_j|}.
    \]
    \item \textbf{Step 4:} Compute the shortest path distances in the graph using Dijkstra's algorithm, based on the weights assigned.
    \item \textbf{Step 5:} Apply a multidimensional scaling method to the set of points, ordering them by the component corresponding to the largest eigenvalue.
    \item \textbf{Step 6:} Once multidimensional scaling has produced one of the two mirror-symmetric solutions, we use the signed RP convention in Equation~\ref{eq:FormulaRP} to select the orientation that best matches the encoded image. This is an empirical orientation check under the fixed reference direction \(v=(1,1)^\top/\sqrt{2}\), not a proof of global bijectivity for the RGB-normalized representation. The process is as follows:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Step 6.1:} Append the mean value of the reconstructed series. This adjustment is necessary because the reconstructed series contains \(N-1\) elements, where \(N\) is the length of the original series, as a consequence of the new RP definition.
        \item \textbf{Step 6.2 (Optional):} Rescale the reconstructed time series using a predefined lookup table, but only if the range values of the original time series are available.
        \item \textbf{Step 6.3:} Generate two potential reconstructions: \(rec_1\) as the direct reconstruction and \(rec_2 = rec_1 \times (-1)\). For each reconstruction, compute the associated signed RP distance matrix using Equation~\ref{eq:FormulaRP} and the same normalization convention used for the original RP image.
        \item \textbf{Step 6.4:} Compare the signed RP matrices of both reconstructions with the decoded original signed RP matrix by calculating the Euclidean distance between them; this comparison is performed before any binary thresholding.
        \item \textbf{Step 6.5:} Select the reconstruction with the smaller Euclidean distance as the candidate most consistent with the encoded signed RP matrix.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Step 7:} Perform cubic-spline interpolation on the reconstructed points and resample the interpolated function to restore the original time-series length of \(N\) values, compensating for the initial reduction to \(N-1\) points.
\end{itemize}
This approach does not make RP fully invertible; instead, it makes the main sources of ambiguity explicit. The signed-RP comparison reduces the reflection ambiguity introduced by multidimensional scaling, while interpolation restores the original length \(N\) from the \(N-1\) adjacent-state representation.


\subsection{Gramian Angular Summation Field Reconstruction}
\label{sec:gasf_reconstruction_method}
Unlike the other three decoders in this section, the GASF inverse is \emph{not} a new contribution of this work: it follows the diagonal-inverse strategy used by Huamin et al.~\cite{huamin2020reconstruction} in the 2-D representation-based denoising autoencoder line of work. We re-derive it here in the notation of the rest of the paper and re-implement it inside the same pipeline used by the other three decoders, so the GASF numbers reported later can be read as a tightly controlled prior-art baseline against which the three novel decoders (RP, GADF, MTF) are compared. The contribution of this subsection is therefore the explicit normalisation and quantisation handling that make the inverse stable when the GASF matrix is read back from an 8-bit PNG, not the inverse map itself.

The GASF decoder is the most direct inverse considered in the completed experiments because its main diagonal contains a sample-wise angular constraint. Under the branch used in this work, the encoded values are first interpreted through
\[
G^{\mathrm{S}}_{ii}=\cos(2\phi_i),
\]
and the angle is recovered as
\[
\hat{\phi}_i=\frac{1}{2}\arccos\left(G^{\mathrm{S}}_{ii}\right).
\]
The normalized signal is then obtained by
\[
\hat{\tilde{x}}_i=\cos(\hat{\phi}_i).
\]
This rule is exact only when the normalization and angular branch are consistent with the encoder. If the full interval \([-1,1]\) is used without additional sign information, the diagonal determines \(|\tilde{x}_i|\) rather than \(\tilde{x}_i\) itself. Therefore, the high numerical accuracy reported for GASF should be read as the result of a controlled normalization protocol, not as an unconditional inverse for every possible GAF image.

The final physical-scale reconstruction uses the same min--max metadata required by the other decoders:
\[
\hat{x}_i=x_{\min}+\frac{\hat{\tilde{x}}_i-\min(\hat{\tilde{x}})}{\max(\hat{\tilde{x}})-\min(\hat{\tilde{x}})}(x_{\max}-x_{\min}).
\]
This step explains why GASF is highly accurate for encoded real windows while still requiring caution in generative settings: a generated image must be accompanied by plausible scale metadata before it can be mapped back to accelerometer units.


\subsection{Restricted-Branch Gramian Angular Difference Field Reconstruction}
\label{sec:gadf_reconstruction_proposal}
We now describe a restricted-branch GADF decoder. Wang and Oates note that GADF can admit an accurate inverse map when the data are rescaled to \([0,1]\) \cite{wang2015encoding}, but their reconstruction discussion operationalises the inverse through the GASF diagonal and does not specify a GADF decoding algorithm. In particular, GADF stores \(\sin(\phi_i-\phi_j)\), so an operational inverse must choose an \(\arcsin\) branch, fix the otherwise free global angular offset, keep a sign convention fixed, and map the recovered angular trajectory back to physical units. The contribution here is therefore not the abstract observation that GADF may be invertible under a restricted angular range, but a fully specified operational decoder: a closed-form pivot-row reconstruction with an explicit branch condition, an image-derived angular anchor, a fixed sign convention, external min--max calibration, and empirical evaluation under the same protocol as RP, MTF, and GASF. It should not be read as a generic inverse for every standard GADF image on the full \([-1,1]\) range. The novelty lies in two coupled design choices: (i) we shrink the normalised amplitude range to $[-\alpha,\alpha]$ with $\alpha=0.7$ so that \emph{every} pairwise angular difference is forced inside the principal branch of $\arcsin$, which is what turns the inverse from an iterative optimisation into a one-pass closed-form computation, and (ii) we anchor the otherwise-relative angular trajectory by reading the angular boundary directly off the extreme row of the GADF image (rather than from an external time-domain index), so that the decoder remains usable when only the image and its stored $(x_{\min},x_{\max})$ metadata are available.

GADF requires a reconstruction strategy different from the GASF diagonal inverse. The diagonal of \(G^{\mathrm{D}}\) is identically zero, but the full matrix stores relative angular information through \(G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ij}=\sin(\phi_i-\phi_j)\). The proposed decoder uses this off-diagonal structure and turns a row of the GADF matrix into a set of recoverable angular differences.

\subsubsection{Restricted scaling for branch uniqueness}
The general GADF inverse is not unique without additional assumptions because \(\sin(\theta)=\sin(\pi-\theta)\). The key design decision is therefore to restrict the normalized amplitude range before encoding. This sacrifices part of the nominal \([-1,1]\) GAF range, but it keeps all pairwise angular differences inside the principal branch of \(\arcsin\), which is what makes a closed-form decoder possible. The input series is scaled as
\[
\bar{x}_t = 1.4\frac{x_t-x_{\min}}{x_{\max}-x_{\min}}-0.7, \qquad \bar{x}_t\in[-\alpha,\alpha],\quad \alpha=0.7.
\]
The corresponding angles satisfy
\[
\phi_t=\arccos(\bar{x}_t)\in[\arccos(\alpha),\arccos(-\alpha)]\approx[0.795,2.346].
\]
The value \(\alpha=0.7\) is not an arbitrary hyperparameter. It is chosen as a near-maximal symmetric amplitude range that preserves the injectivity of the inverse-sine step. For the closed-form GADF inverse to be unique, every angular difference must remain inside the principal branch of \(\arcsin\):
\[
|\phi_i-\phi_j|<\frac{\pi}{2}.
\]
The worst-case angular spread is
\[
\arccos(-\alpha)-\arccos(\alpha).
\]
Using \(\arccos(-\alpha)=\pi-\arccos(\alpha)\), the uniqueness condition becomes
\[
\pi-2\arccos(\alpha)<\frac{\pi}{2},
\]
or equivalently
\[
\alpha<\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\approx0.7071.
\]
We therefore set \(\alpha=0.7\), close to the largest admissible symmetric range while leaving a small numerical margin:
\[
|\phi_i-\phi_j|\leq \arccos(-0.7)-\arccos(0.7)\approx 1.551 < \frac{\pi}{2}.
\]
This keeps every angular difference within the principal branch of \(\arcsin\). Under this restriction,
\[
\arcsin\left(G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ij}\right)=\phi_i-\phi_j.
\]
Without the restriction \(\alpha<1/\sqrt{2}\), the same value \(\sin(\phi_i-\phi_j)\) could correspond to multiple angular-difference configurations, and the GADF matrix would no longer determine the angular differences uniquely. The branch restriction gives a single angular reconstruction under the controlled protocol, once the absolute angular anchor and the min--max scale metadata are also fixed. A complete robustness study should still sweep \(\alpha\), quantify anchor-selection failures, and separate floating-point reconstruction error from 8-bit PNG quantisation error.

\subsubsection{Pivot-row angular reconstruction}
For a pivot row \(p\), the GADF entries provide
\[
G^{\mathrm{D}}_{pj}=\sin(\phi_p-\phi_j),
\]
and, by the branch condition above,
\[
\phi_j=\phi_p-\arcsin\left(G^{\mathrm{D}}_{pj}\right),\qquad j=1,\ldots,N.
\]
Thus, a single absolute pivot angle \(\phi_p\) determines the entire angular trajectory. The pivot is chosen from the image itself rather than from an external time-domain index, because a stored or generated GADF image may not preserve the original contextual information. We select the pivot row as
\[
p=\arg\min_i \left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ij}\right|,
\]
which chooses the row whose average signed angular difference is closest to zero. Because \(G^{\mathrm{D}}\) is anti-symmetric, this row is typically close to the angular center of the series and reduces the maximum magnitude of the recovered differences.

\subsubsection{Absolute angular anchor}
The pivot row reconstructs angles relative to \(\phi_p\), so one absolute angular anchor is still required. In the restricted scaling protocol, the original minimum and maximum are stored as calibration metadata. This requirement is not a cosmetic implementation detail: without a scale anchor, GADF determines relative angular shape but not physical acceleration units. Because the normalization is performed per window, the encoded normalized signal necessarily reaches the two boundary values \(+\alpha\) and \(-\alpha\) unless the window is constant. The maximum and minimum samples therefore provide natural angular anchors:
\[
\phi_{\max}=\arccos(\alpha), \qquad \phi_{\min}=\arccos(-\alpha).
\]
Operationally, the boundary row is identified from the image as the extreme row
\[
e=\arg\max_i \left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ij}\right|.
\]
The sign of the mean row indicates which boundary is represented: a positive mean corresponds to the large-angle boundary \(\phi_e=\arccos(-\alpha)=\phi_{\min}\), whereas a negative mean corresponds to the small-angle boundary \(\phi_e=\arccos(\alpha)=\phi_{\max}\). Once one of these boundary rows is identified, the rest of the angular trajectory follows directly from
\[
\phi_j=\phi_e-\arcsin\left(G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ej}\right),\qquad j=1,\ldots,N,
\]
under the restricted-branch condition, and the pivot angle follows as the special case
\[
\phi_p=\phi_e-\arcsin\left(G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ep}\right).
\]
The reconstruction is therefore unique within the controlled protocol: the GADF supplies all pairwise angular differences, while the boundary value imposed by the per-window normalization fixes the otherwise free global angular offset.
Finally, the normalized series is recovered as
\[
\hat{\bar{x}}_j=\cos(\phi_j),\qquad j=1,\ldots,N.
\]
If the stored matrix uses the transposed GADF convention \(\sin(\phi_j-\phi_i)\), the signs in the two equations above are reversed; the implementation must therefore keep the convention in Equation~\ref{eq:gadf_indexed} fixed.

\subsubsection{Denormalization and limitations}
The GADF image determines the shape of the normalized trajectory under the restricted branch assumption, but it does not by itself recover the physical scale. The final denormalization uses the calibration pair \((x_{\min},x_{\max})\):
\[
\hat{x}_j=x_{\min}+\frac{\hat{\bar{x}}_j+\alpha}{2\alpha}(x_{\max}-x_{\min}), \qquad \alpha=0.7.
\]
The method is therefore closed-form and linear in \(N\) after row statistics have been computed, but it is not universally invertible. It relies on the restricted scaling interval, a consistent GADF sign convention, the presence or reliable identification of an angular boundary, and external min--max metadata. Stating these requirements is essential for the paper's scope: the decoder is a controlled inverse map, not a guarantee that every visually plausible GADF image corresponds to a valid physical signal. When these assumptions fail, a more general nonlinear least-squares reconstruction can be used:
\[
\min_{\phi}\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}\left(\sin(\phi_i-\phi_j)-G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ij}\right)^2,
\]
with one angle fixed to remove the global phase ambiguity and with smoothness or locality weights \(w_{ij}\) for robustness.

\subsection{Markov Transition Field Reconstruction}
\label{sec:mtf_reconstruction_method}
The MTF decoder described below is, to the best of our knowledge, not preceded by a documented explicit MTF-to-time-series decoder in the literature reviewed here. The decoder is not bijective: a Markov chain induces an equivalence class of time series with the same transition statistics, and no algorithm can distinguish elements of that class from the MTF image alone. What follows recovers a representative of that class by clustering the MTF rows into quantile bins and fixing the bin ordering through the Fiedler vector of the recovered transition graph. This spectral ordering step is motivated by the seriation literature, where the Fiedler vector can recover a latent linear order under appropriate structural conditions \cite{Atkins1998}; in our setting, the exact-order statement should therefore be read as an ideal birth--death-chain case, not as a universal guarantee for arbitrary MTF images.

The reconstruction proceeds in four steps:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Step 1: Bin recovery.} Cluster the $T$ rows of the MTF matrix $M\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}$ into $Q$ groups by farthest-point-initialised $k$-means on the row-normalised image. Under a clean MTF every sample in the same quantile bin has the same row pattern, so this recovers the bin label sequence $\{q_1,\ldots,q_T\}$ up to a bin permutation.

    \item \textbf{Step 2: Bin ordering via the Fiedler vector.} Estimate the bin transition matrix $W$ as the average of MTF entries inside every $(p,q)$ block, symmetrise it as $S=\tfrac{1}{2}(W+W^{\top})$, and order the bins by the Fiedler vector of the graph Laplacian $L=D-S$ (the eigenvector corresponding to the second-smallest eigenvalue). For an ideal birth--death Markov chain on $Q$ ordered states this recovers the linear amplitude order; for general chains it is a spectral seriation heuristic whose quality depends on how strongly the recovered transition graph reflects an underlying one-dimensional amplitude order.

    \item \textbf{Step 3: Amplitude assignment.} For every time index $i$ belonging to sorted bin $G_k$, assign the midpoint of the corresponding quantile interval as the reconstructed normalised value:
    \[
    \hat{x}_i=\frac{k-\tfrac{1}{2}}{Q}, \qquad k=1,\ldots,Q.
    \]

    \item \textbf{Step 4: Denormalisation.} Rescale $\hat{X}$ using the min--max calibration $(x_{\min},x_{\max})$ stored at encoding time:
    \[
    x'_i = x_{\min} + \hat{x}_i\,(x_{\max}-x_{\min}), \qquad i=1,\ldots,T.
    \]
\end{itemize}

The procedure does not verify the global sign of the series, because the MTF representation emphasises transition structure rather than signed amplitude order; a single sign-bit per axis (not stored here) would resolve the remaining mirror ambiguity. We use $Q=4$ bins throughout the reported experiments. The choice is defended both by a noise-of-estimator argument -- with $T=128$ samples and $Q$ bins there are $\sim (T-1)/Q^{2}$ transitions per cell of the empirical row-stochastic matrix $W$, so $Q=4$ admits $\sim 8$ transitions per cell while $Q=8$ drops to $\sim 2$ and many cells of $W$ become degenerate at $0$ or $1$ -- and empirically by the bin-count ablation in Section~\ref{sec:mtf_ablation}, where $Q=4$ is the unique value that simultaneously minimises the pointwise MAE, maximises the pointwise Pearson correlation, and maximises the accuracy of the two classifiers most sensitive to amplitude detail (KNN and CNN). The remaining four classifiers (SVM, Random Forest, LSTM, GRU) vary by at most $0.010$ accuracy across the entire sweep, so the recommendation of $Q=4$ is dominated by the amplitude-sensitive subset rather than overridden by the others.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:experimental_protocol}
The experimental protocol is designed to answer three separate questions: whether the signal can be reconstructed point-by-point, whether its global trend is preserved, and whether the reconstructed window still supports activity recognition. These questions are intentionally separated because a representation may be poor for sample-level reconstruction while still retaining enough coarse structure for classification. This section fixes the dataset, segmentation, qualitative checks, metrics, and classifier protocol before the results are reported.

\subsection{Dataset and Segmentation}
The dataset used is the \textit{WISDM Smartphone and Smartwatch Activity and Biometrics Dataset} \cite{misc_wisdm_smartphone_and_smartwatch_activity_and_biometrics_dataset__507}. It contains data from 51 subjects, with accelerometer and gyroscope measurements collected from a smartwatch worn on the dominant hand and a mobile device kept in the pocket. The dataset includes 18 activities, each recorded for 3 minutes. We use the smartwatch accelerometer stream, which is sampled at 20\,Hz (i.e., one sample every 50\,ms).

For our experiments, we selected five common activities with nearly balanced sample counts. This subset keeps the classification task interpretable while avoiding a strongly imbalanced evaluation in which reconstruction effects could be masked by class-frequency effects. The chosen activities are:

We segmented the data into non-overlapping windows (FNOW) of length \(N=128\), corresponding to 6.4\,seconds at 20\,Hz. The window length is fixed at a power of two because it is the de-facto standard for convolutional/recurrent backbones used in human-activity recognition, and because it keeps the GASF, GADF, and MTF images at \(128\times128\) pixels. For the modified RP formulation, adjacent states are still written as \(s_j=(x_j,x_{j+1})\), so a length-\(N\) window yields an \((N-1)\times(N-1)=127\times127\) image; the reconstruction pipeline then augments the recovered \(N-1\) samples with the per-window mean (step 6.1 of the Hirata--Horai--Aihara adaptation) so that the final signal matches the 128-sample window length used by the other encodings. With \(N_{\text{bins}}=4\), the MTF partition contains four equal-size groups of \(N/Q=32\) time indices, which exactly tiles the window with no leftover index.

By decomposing the X, Y, and Z dimensions into individual time series of size 128, we obtain 8\,227 windows per channel, resulting in a total of $8\,227 \times 3 = 24\,681$ one-dimensional reconstructions for the reconstruction metrics. For classification, however, the experimental unit is the full triaxial window: the three reconstructed axes are grouped back into a single sample, yielding 8\,227 labeled windows. The LOTO protocol assigns these to three folds of $\sim$2\,742 test windows each, so the pooled paired-sample size used by the inferential tests in Section~\ref{sec:statistical_protocol} is $N=8\,227$.

\subsection{Evaluation Design}

We evaluated the four reconstruction pipelines with a three-tier design. The first tier measures sample-level fidelity, the second tier measures trend-level similarity, and the third tier measures downstream discriminative utility. This structure is important because the three questions are not equivalent: a method can have large pointwise errors but still preserve enough temporal structure for a classifier, or it can reconstruct amplitudes accurately while losing information that matters for a specific task. Prior work on regression error metrics and time-series similarity supports using complementary measures when each metric is tied to a specific interpretation \cite{Botchkarev_2019,Ding2008}.

In the first tier, MAE, MRE, MSE, and Pearson correlation are used to compare the original and reconstructed samples directly. MAE gives an interpretable average absolute deviation, MSE penalizes large local failures, MRE normalizes error by signal magnitude, and Pearson correlation measures whether the temporal pattern is linearly aligned even when amplitudes differ. No single metric is sufficient, so these values are interpreted together rather than collapsed into one score.

In the second tier, each min--max normalized window is summarized by a global cubic polynomial. The coefficient vector is \((a_3,a_2,a_1,a_0)\) in the standard power basis, not a piecewise cubic-spline representation. This choice intentionally discards local sample-level detail and tests whether the reconstructed signal preserves broad trend shape. Euclidean and Manhattan distances measure coefficient magnitude differences, cosine similarity measures directional agreement, and Mahalanobis distance accounts for the covariance structure of the original coefficient vectors \cite{Shirkhorshidi2015}.

In the third tier, we measure original-trained classifier robustness to reconstructed inputs. Each classifier is trained once on the original triaxial windows of the training split and then evaluated, without retraining, on the original and reconstructed versions of the same held-out windows. Classification is included because reconstructed signals should retain task-relevant information, but this experiment measures distribution shift relative to a raw-signal classifier; it is not a direct augmentation experiment. \subsubsection{Statistical Significance Protocol}
\label{sec:statistical_protocol}
Every comparison reported in this paper is paired. For each LOTO fold, the four reconstructions and the original signal are evaluated on the same $\sim$2\,742 test windows, and each classifier is fit once per fold and reused across the five sources within that fold. Per-fold paired correctness vectors are then concatenated across folds 0, 1 and 2, yielding a pooled paired sample of $N=8\,227$ windows that drives all the inferential tests. Pooling is statistically valid here because each window is a single paired observation -- it never appears both in training and in test within the same fold, and the test set of a fold never contains a window from any other fold's test set.

For \emph{accuracy reporting}, every accuracy cell in the per-classifier tables (Tables~\ref{tab:gru_classifiers}, \ref{tab:knn_classifiers}, \ref{tab:cnn_classifiers}, \ref{tab:rf_classifiers}, \ref{tab:svm_classifiers}, and~\ref{tab:lstm_classifiers}) is the mean of the three fold accuracies and the dispersion is reported as the standard deviation across folds. Fold-level dispersion is a more honest measure of generalisation than a within-fold bootstrap, which only captures sampling noise inside a single split.

For \emph{paired classifier comparisons} we use the continuity-corrected McNemar test on the pooled discordant pairs \cite{Dietterich1998} -- the standard test when two classifiers are evaluated on the same instances -- and we distinguish two test families:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{F\textsubscript{M1}: Original vs.\ reconstruction.} For every one of the 4 reconstructions (RP, MTF, GASF, GADF) and every one of the 6 classifiers we test whether the paired correctness vector differs from the original-signal baseline. This produces $4\times 6=24$ paired tests. These McNemar tests are degradation/difference tests, not formal equivalence tests; absence of significance is therefore reported as ``no detected degradation'' rather than proof of equivalence.
\item \textbf{F\textsubscript{M2}: encoder vs.\ encoder at fixed classifier.} For every pair of reconstructions and every classifier we ask which encoder yields the higher accuracy under that classifier. There are $\binom{4}{2}=6$ encoder pairs and 6 classifiers, hence $6\times 6=36$ paired tests.
\end{itemize}

For \emph{paired pointwise-error comparisons} between encoders we use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test on per-window MAE \cite{Wilcoxon1945}. This test compares two reconstructions on the same windows and does not involve a classifier, so the family contains only $\binom{4}{2}=6$ tests (F\textsubscript{W}). Alongside each Wilcoxon $p$-value we report a non-parametric effect size $r=Z/\sqrt{N}$, where $|r|\geq 0.5$ is conventionally read as a large effect \cite{Rosenthal1991}. For the specific claim that \emph{GASF correlates with the original significantly more strongly than GADF does}, the two Pearson correlations are dependent because they share the same vector of originals; we therefore use Steiger's z for dependent correlations \cite{Steiger1980}.

Because the three families F\textsubscript{M1}, F\textsubscript{M2} and F\textsubscript{W} contain $24$, $36$ and $6$ simultaneous tests respectively, raw $p$-values within each family are corrected with the Holm--Bonferroni step-down procedure \cite{Holm1979} at a family-wise error rate $\alpha=0.05$. Throughout the paper we report the Holm-adjusted value $p_{\text{Holm}}$ and call a comparison significant only when $p_{\text{Holm}}<\alpha$ inside its own family; the unadjusted $p$-values are not used to declare significance.

\subsubsection{Pointwise Error Metrics}

To evaluate the reconstruction methods, we use the following error metrics:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mean Absolute Error (MAE):} This metric represents the average of the absolute differences between the original and reconstructed values. It provides a direct measure of the magnitude of the errors without considering their direction.

  \item \textbf{Mean Relative Error (MRE):} Calculated as
  \[
  \operatorname{MRE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{|x_i-\hat{x}_i|}{|x_i|+\varepsilon},
  \]
  where \(\varepsilon=10^{-8}\) prevents division by zero when accelerometer values are zero or close to zero. This metric normalizes the absolute error by the magnitude of the original signal.

  \item \textbf{Mean Squared Error (MSE):} By squaring each error before averaging, the MSE gives a higher penalty to larger errors, thereby emphasizing large deviations in the reconstruction.

  \item \textbf{Pearson Correlation Coefficient:} This statistical measure indicates the degree of linear correlation between the original and reconstructed signals. A coefficient close to 1 indicates that the reconstructed signal follows the original temporal pattern.
\end{itemize}

These metrics are computed for each accelerometer axis (Dim 0, Dim 1, and Dim 2) and then aggregated as a global mean, allowing axis-level errors and overall reconstruction quality to be reported separately for RP, MTF, GASF, and GADF.


\subsubsection{Polynomial Trend Metrics}
\label{sec:polynomial_trend_metrics}

To evaluate the performance of the polynomial representation reconstruction, the following normalized metrics were computed on the cubic coefficient vectors. Each coefficient dimension was standardized using the mean and standard deviation estimated from the original windows before distances were calculated. For Mahalanobis distance, the covariance matrix was estimated from the standardized coefficient vectors of the original windows and regularized as \(\Sigma_{\lambda}=\Sigma+10^{-6}I\) before inversion.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mean Euclidean Distance:}
    This metric calculates the average Euclidean distance between the standardized cubic coefficient vectors of the original and reconstructed signals. A lower value indicates a more accurate reconstruction.

    \item \textbf{Mean Manhattan Distance:}
    This metric represents the mean absolute difference (L1 norm) between the standardized cubic coefficient vectors, and it is sensitive to the overall magnitude of the differences.

    \item \textbf{Mean Cosine Similarity:}
    This metric evaluates the cosine of the angle between the standardized coefficient vectors, yielding a value close to 1 when the reconstructed polynomial follows the same direction as the original, regardless of magnitude differences.

    \item \textbf{Mean Mahalanobis Distance:}
    This metric measures the distance between the standardized coefficient vectors using \(\Sigma_{\lambda}^{-1}\), taking into account the variance and correlation structure estimated from the original coefficient vectors. It provides a multidimensional assessment of the similarity between the original and reconstructed polynomials.
\end{itemize}

The quantitative results for these metrics are presented in Table~\ref{tab:normalized_metrics} later in the document.
\subsubsection{Classifier Evaluation}

To test whether reconstructed windows retain task-relevant information, we evaluated them with several classifiers. These classifiers do not prove pointwise fidelity; instead, they indicate whether class-discriminative temporal structure remains available after reconstruction. The selection of classifiers was inspired by Ignatov et al. \cite{IGNATOV2018915}, who analyzed multiple classifiers on accelerometer-based activity recognition.

Specifically, we used a three-fold protocol with \(n=3\) partitions. Each fold holds out one partition for testing and uses the remaining two partitions for training, producing a 66.6\%/33.3\% train-test split per fold. The 8,227 instances correspond to triaxial windows, not to separate one-dimensional axes; therefore, each fold contains approximately 2,742 test windows. Reported metrics represent the average performance obtained across the three folds when comparing the original time series against their reconstructed counterparts.

A detailed overview of each classifier and its intended role is presented below:

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU):}
A recurrent neural network variant designed to effectively capture temporal dependencies in sequential data. Its gating mechanisms regulate the information flow, enabling recognition of complex patterns over time.

\item \textbf{K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN):}
An instance-based classifier that assigns labels based on the majority class of the closest $k$ neighbors. It acts as a baseline to determine how well separated the reconstructed classes are within the feature space.

\item \textbf{Convolutional Neural Network (CNN):}
A deep neural network architecture optimized for extracting local temporal patterns and spatial relationships through convolutional filters. CNN effectively identifies discriminative motifs critical for activity classification.

\item \textbf{Random Forest (RF):}
An ensemble classifier comprising multiple decision trees that independently vote to determine the final class prediction. RF robustly captures nonlinear relationships and interactions among features.

\item \textbf{Support Vector Machine (SVM):}
This classifier finds separating hyperplanes in high-dimensional feature spaces and tests whether reconstructed windows remain separable under a margin-based model.

\item \textbf{Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM):}
A recurrent neural network designed to model long-term dependencies in sequential data. Its gating mechanisms test whether longer temporal correlations remain available after reconstruction.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Grouped Subject-Out Robustness}
\label{sec:loso_results}

The LOTO 3-fold protocol used so far holds out individual trials but allows windows from the same subject to appear in both the training and the test split of a fold. This is the standard configuration in the WISDM literature and the right test for whether a reconstruction preserves task-relevant temporal structure. It is not, however, a test of whether the model generalises to \emph{unseen subjects} -- the regime that matters most for wearable deployment. We therefore replicate the full evaluation under a grouped subject-out 3-fold protocol: the 51 WISDM subjects are partitioned by StratifiedGroupKFold into three disjoint groups of 16, 18 and 17 test subjects respectively, and the test set of one fold never shares a subject with either of its training folds. We use the term subject-out rather than strict leave-one-subject-out, since each fold holds out a disjoint group of subjects rather than a single subject. Encoding, reconstruction, and the six-classifier panel are exactly the same as for the LOTO protocol; only the train/test indices change. Each subject-out fold contains $\sim$2\,742 test windows so the pooled paired-sample size for inferential tests is again $N=8\,227$.

\input{_loso_tables.tex}

Three observations follow from Tables~\ref{tab:gadf_summary_loso}, \ref{tab:gadf_summary_perfold_loso}, and~\ref{tab:loto_vs_loso}:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The encoder ranking is preserved under subject-out evaluation, including the RP/MTF lens inversion of Section~\ref{sec:disc_classification}.} This is the load-bearing robustness check for the structural argument: the LOTO orderings are not artefacts of allowing the model to memorise per-subject calibration. Under grouped subject-out validation the absolute accuracies drop by $0.08$--$0.16$ as expected (the model has never seen the test subjects), but $\text{GASF}\succ\text{GADF}$ holds on every individual classifier of every fold, and the RP/MTF inversion observed under LOTO -- MTF beats RP on five of the six classifiers, KNN being the single exception -- replicates exactly under subject-out validation ($p_{\text{Holm}}<10^{-10}$ on SVM, RF, LSTM, CNN, GRU for MTF$\succ$RP; KNN is the exception that keeps RP$\succ$MTF). The lens-dependent ranking is therefore not a within-trial artefact.
\item \textbf{GASF shows no detected degradation from the original under this paired test.} The paired McNemar test on the pooled subject-out sample fails to reject Original-vs-GASF in all six classifiers ($p_{\text{Holm}}\in[0.30,1.00]$); the absolute subject-out accuracy drop of GASF relative to the original is bounded by $|\Delta\text{acc}|\leq 0.001$ across the six classifiers, identical to the LOTO behaviour. This replicates the near-equivalence pattern of Section~\ref{sec:disc_classification}, while still stopping short of a formal equivalence or information-preservation claim.
\item \textbf{Under subject-out validation, GADF is the only reconstruction that strictly improves on the original for one classifier.} On KNN, GADF reaches $0.500\pm0.017$ while the original signal only reaches $0.477\pm0.010$ ($+0.022$ acc, paired McNemar $p_{\text{Holm}}=6.4\!\times\!10^{-11}$). RP-KNN shows no detected difference from the original under subject-out validation ($p_{\text{Holm}}=1.0$). This result is consistent with a regularization or low-pass hypothesis: the same smoothing that costs GADF $0.07$--$0.10$ accuracy on high-ceiling classifiers under LOTO becomes beneficial for the instance-based KNN classifier under the harder subject-out split. However, a dedicated frequency-domain analysis -- e.g., original vs.\ GADF/GASF power spectral density, high-frequency energy ratios, and smoothed-original baselines such as moving average, Savitzky--Golay or Butterworth filters -- is required before interpreting GADF as a denoising mechanism.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{MTF Decoder Ablation: Ordering and Metadata}
\label{sec:mtf_ablation}

The MTF decoder has two design choices we need to defend empirically: the bin-ordering algorithm (cosine-pivot heuristic vs Fiedler-vector spectral) and the bin count $Q$. We ablate both on the LOTO 3-fold protocol against the same six classifiers used in the rest of the paper.

\paragraph*{Decoder ablation (at fixed $Q=4$).}
We compare four decoder configurations on the same encoded MTF images (Table~\ref{tab:mtf_ablation}): the cosine-pivot ordering heuristic, Fiedler-vector ordering, Fiedler-vector ordering with stored bin-midpoint metadata, and Fiedler-vector ordering with both midpoint metadata and a sign-bit. The encoder is held constant. Bin midpoints (storing $Q-1$ floats per axis to recover the true median of each quantile bin) and a sign-bit (one bit per axis to disambiguate the mirror symmetry) are \emph{not} part of the side-information contract used for the headline numbers; they characterize the achievable floor that any MTF decoder operating on top of the standard MTF image cannot beat.

\end{enumerate}

Taken together, the two ablations defend the $Q=4$ default empirically: it is the unique value that minimises pointwise MAE, maximises pointwise Pearson, and maximises both amplitude-sensitive classifier accuracies (KNN and CNN), while costing at most $0.010$ accuracy on the four classifiers that are essentially flat across $Q$.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The four encodings have now been measured under the same WISDM protocol -- $N=128$ windows, full LOTO 3-fold evaluation ($N=8\,227$ pooled test windows; $\sim$2\,742 per fold), six classifiers fit \emph{once per fold} on the originals and evaluated on every reconstruction -- so the comparison no longer mixes one fully evaluated method against partially evaluated ones and is no longer restricted to a single split. We organise the discussion around four lenses (visual reconstruction, pointwise error, polynomial-trend similarity, and downstream classification) and ask, in turn, where the four encodings sit on each lens and whether the rankings agree. \subsection{Visual Reconstruction Trade-offs}
\label{sec:disc_visual}

Figures~\ref{fig:rp_comparison_wisdm}--\ref{fig:mtf_comparison_global} show the four reconstructions overlaid on the original triaxial window. GASF (Fig.~\ref{fig:gaf_comparison_wisdm}) is visually indistinguishable from the original on all three axes, including high-frequency wiggles, confirming that the diagonal half-angle rule combined with the stored min--max recovers both shape and amplitude. GADF (Fig.~\ref{fig:gadf_comparison_wisdm}) preserves the global trend and the dominant oscillation but smooths short-range variation; this is the signature of a closed-form decoder that depends on a single pivot row and on the 8-bit PNG quantisation. RP (Fig.~\ref{fig:rp_comparison_wisdm}) recovers the dominant morphology but exhibits amplitude bias and occasional sign uncertainty: the classical-MDS embedding is deterministic but still leaves a mirror ambiguity that the signed-RP convention resolves only when the orientation comparison is decisive. MTF (Fig.~\ref{fig:mtf_comparison_global}) tracks coarse structure but reduces the signal to a small number of plateaus, the unavoidable consequence of representing every sample by a $Q=4$ state label.

\subsection{Null-Corrected RP/GADF Regional Overlap}
\label{sec:disc_rp_gadf_overlap}

On all \(8\,227\) non-overlapping smartwatch-accelerometer WISDM windows available under the \(N=128\) raw-window protocol, we encode the same signal as signed RP and GADF, align the matrices on the common \(127\times127\) support, and quantise each image channel into \(B\) visual intensity ranges. For every matched pair \((\mathrm{RP}_i,\mathrm{GADF}_i)\), we compare the quantised regions against a conservative null pair \((\mathrm{RP}_i,\mathrm{GADF}_j)\), where \(j\neq i\) is drawn from the same activity class. \subsection{Reconstruction Error Analysis}
\label{sec:disc_pointwise}

The pointwise tables (Tables~\ref{tab:recurrence_plot}, \ref{tab:markov_fields}, \ref{tab:grammian_fields}, \ref{tab:gadf_fields}) confirm the visual ordering. GASF attains a global MAE of $0.0209$ and a Pearson correlation of $0.99988 \pm 0.00020$; the residual error is on the order of $1/255$, which matches the resolution of the 8-bit RGB intermediate and rules out a structural failure of the diagonal inverse. GADF reaches $\text{MAE}=1.47 \pm 1.65$ and Pearson $0.983 \pm 0.025$, roughly $2.0\times$ tighter than RP and $2.7\times$ tighter than MTF in MAE: the pivot-row scheme propagates the cumulative error of the chosen anchor row, but for windows where some row has near-zero mean GADF this propagation is mild. RP yields $\text{MAE}=2.95 \pm 3.23$ and Pearson $0.747 \pm 0.175$; the standard deviations across windows are large because the sign-flip check works decisively on some windows and is borderline on others. MTF under the Fiedler-vector decoder of Section~\ref{sec:mtf_ablation} reaches $\text{MAE}=4.00 \pm 6.31$, $\text{MSE}=56.10 \pm 205.03$ and a Pearson correlation that averages to $0.017 \pm 0.837$. The wide $\pm 0.84$ spread is the diagnostic of the residual mirror ambiguity: per window, MTF correlates either strongly positively or strongly negatively with the original because the recovered amplitude order can land on the wrong side of the global flip, and the two outcomes cancel in the mean.

The four pointwise orderings are statistically significant under paired, distribution-free testing. Wilcoxon signed-rank on the per-window MAE pooled across the 3 LOTO folds ($N=8\,227$ paired windows) rejects equality between every pair of encoders ($p_{\text{Holm}}<10^{-200}$ for all six pairs). Five of the six effect sizes $|r|=Z/\sqrt{N}$ are in the large-effect regime under the Rosenthal convention ($|r|\in\{0.85, 0.85, 0.87, 0.87, 0.87\}$ for the GASF-, GADF- and MTF-vs-MTF/RP comparisons that pit a closed-form decoder against a heuristic one); the sixth, RP-vs-MTF, is a medium effect ($r=-0.351$) because the Fiedler MTF decoder closes most of the pointwise gap between RP and MTF that the cosine-pivot decoder had opened. The sign of $r$ still recovers the ranking $\text{GASF}\succ\text{GADF}\succ\text{RP}\succ\text{MTF}$ on a per-window pointwise basis, but the RP-vs-MTF gap is the narrowest one and -- as Section~\ref{sec:disc_classification} below shows -- it is the gap that inverts when the lens changes from pointwise error to downstream classification accuracy.

\subsection{Polynomial Trend Comparison}
\label{sec:disc_trend}

Compressing each min--max-normalised window into a global cubic-polynomial coefficient vector $(a_3,a_2,a_1,a_0)$ removes most sample-level noise and asks only whether the broad shape is right. The result (Table~\ref{tab:normalized_metrics}, computed with the standardized-coefficient distance metrics defined in Section~\ref{sec:polynomial_trend_metrics}) reorders nothing among the closed-form decoders: GASF first (cosine similarity $0.99979$), GADF second ($0.99550$), RP third ($0.90393$), MTF last ($0.88732$). Two observations matter: (i) RP's polynomial cosine similarity is much higher than its pointwise Pearson, which is consistent with our reading that RP captures shape but mis-scales amplitude, and (ii) MTF's polynomial cosine similarity is competitive with RP even though its pointwise correlation collapses to zero, supporting the view that MTF retains a usable rough trend even when it loses local amplitude -- a property that the Fiedler-vector spectral decoder of Section~\ref{sec:mtf_ablation} leverages explicitly into a classifier advantage (Section~\ref{sec:disc_classification}).

\subsection{Classification Performance}
\label{sec:disc_classification}

The six per-classifier tables preserve the GASF/GADF top tier but also reveal the RP/MTF inversion and where reconstructions are good enough to be downstream-equivalent.

GASF reconstructions show no statistically significant degradation relative to the originals across all six classifiers ($|\Delta\text{acc}|\leq 0.001$ on the 3-fold mean for KNN, SVM, LSTM, Random Forest, CNN, and GRU). The paired McNemar test \cite{Dietterich1998} confirms this on the pooled $N=8\,227$ windows: in every one of the six Original-vs-GASF comparisons the raw $p$-value lies between $0.11$ and $0.75$ and the Holm-adjusted value is between $0.68$ and $1.0$, so we do not detect a paired classification difference under this protocol (24-test family, $\alpha=0.05$). This is not a formal equivalence test: failing to reject a difference is not, by itself, proof of equivalence or information preservation. Combined with the near-zero pointwise error, it supports the narrower claim that GASF is empirically near-equivalent for the evaluated WISDM task under controlled encoding and stored min--max metadata. A formal equivalence claim would require a pre-specified margin, such as a TOST-style test or confidence interval on \(\Delta\)accuracy.

GADF loses 0.07--0.10 accuracy on the four high-ceiling classifiers (CNN, GRU, LSTM, Random Forest), 0.08 on SVM, and only 0.01 on KNN (3-fold means). The paired McNemar test rejects equality with the originals for all six classifiers under pooled $N=8\,227$ ($p_{\text{Holm}}<10^{-59}$ for SVM, LSTM, Random Forest, CNN and GRU; KNN rejects with $p_{\text{Holm}}=1.7\!\times\!10^{-2}$, the narrowest margin of the six). The same McNemar family confirms that GADF beats RP for every classifier ($p_{\text{Holm}}<10^{-9}$ in all six pairs), consolidating GADF's second place. The KNN figure is still the smallest gap: KNN measures local geometric similarity in the flattened sample space, so it is the classifier most directly sensitive to the smoothing artefact we observed visually; that it loses the least suggests the GADF smoothing preserves local neighbour relations even when it shrinks high-frequency content.

RP loses 0.18--0.22 accuracy on the high-ceiling classifiers, 0.16 on SVM, and only 0.04 on KNN (3-fold means). The paired McNemar test rejects equality with the originals for all six classifiers ($p_{\text{Holm}}<10^{-17}$, dropping to $p_{\text{Holm}}<10^{-200}$ for SVM, Random Forest, LSTM, GRU and CNN), placing RP firmly in the lossy regime. The RP-KNN accuracy of $0.599\pm0.006$ sits slightly below GADF and is what we read as the genuine ceiling of the Hirata--Horai--Aihara family under the chosen threshold $\rho=0.75$, once the parallel reconstruction is reassembled in sample order so that decoded windows remain aligned with their labels.

MTF, under the Fiedler-vector decoder of Section~\ref{sec:mtf_ablation}, loses 0.13--0.17 accuracy on the high-ceiling classifiers, 0.11 on SVM, and 0.09 on KNN (3-fold means). Two findings about MTF deserve to be highlighted because they reverse the standard intuition that MTF is "good for classification, bad for reconstruction" only in a vague sense, and now make the claim quantitative and statistically signed.

\emph{First, MTF dominates RP on five of the six classifiers.} The paired McNemar test on the pooled $N=8\,227$ sample rejects RP-vs-MTF equality in four classifiers with $p_{\text{Holm}}<10^{-20}$ (SVM, Random Forest, LSTM and CNN), all of them in MTF's favour; the GRU case is the only one where the gap is too small to be flagged at the family-wise 5\% level ($p_{\text{Holm}}=0.067$, mean accuracies $0.745$ vs $0.736$ in MTF's favour). KNN is the single exception that reverses the trend: $\text{RP}=0.599$ vs $\text{MTF}=0.548$, $p_{\text{Holm}}=3.6\!\times\!10^{-23}$. We read this as a structural property of the two encodings: MTF discards within-bin amplitude but preserves quantile-ordered transition statistics, which is precisely the kind of representation a non-instance-based classifier needs; RP preserves amplitude (lower pointwise MAE, as Section~\ref{sec:disc_pointwise} shows) but the noise of the MDS-based reconstruction perturbs neighbourhood geometry, which is what KNN actually measures.

\emph{Second, the rank inversion between pointwise and classifier lenses is the central qualitative finding of this paper's MTF evaluation.} On the pointwise lens the ranking is $\text{GASF}\succ\text{GADF}\succ\text{RP}\succ\text{MTF}$ (Section~\ref{sec:disc_pointwise}); on the classifier lens it is $\text{GASF}\succ\text{GADF}\succ\text{MTF}\succ\text{RP}$ for every classifier except KNN. The two rankings do not contradict each other -- they answer different questions -- and the fact that the inversion happens cleanly along the RP/MTF pair, rather than spreading across the ranking, supports a "classifier-oriented vs reconstruction-oriented representation" reading of MTF (transition statistics) versus RP (recurrence geometry). Per-class behaviour matches that reading: under MTF, classes for which the source signal is amplitude-monotonic (sitting, standing) survive the $Q=4$ quantisation reasonably well, and classes that depend on amplitude shape (jogging, stairs) lose precision but retain recall through the preserved transition order.

\subsection{Four-lens Convergence and the RP/MTF Inversion}
\label{sec:disc_convergence}

Aggregating across the four lenses we observe a partial convergence with one principled inversion. The top of the ranking is stable on every lens: $\text{GASF}\succ\text{GADF}$ holds for visual reconstruction, pointwise error, polynomial-trend similarity, and downstream classification, and the gap GASF--GADF is statistically significant in every case (Sections~\ref{sec:disc_pointwise} and~\ref{sec:disc_classification}). The proximity between RP and GADF on directional pairwise structure -- discussed in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} -- is also visible in every lens: GADF is strictly antisymmetric, while signed RP is a zero-diagonal directional distance matrix whose external sign rule approximates a related pairwise-difference structure without guaranteeing exact antisymmetry.

The interesting feature is the RP/MTF pair, which inverts between lenses. On the pointwise lens RP is closer to the original than MTF ($\text{MAE}=2.95$ vs $4.00$; Wilcoxon $r=-0.351$); on the polynomial-trend lens RP is also slightly ahead (cosine similarity $0.904$ vs $0.887$); but on the classifier lens MTF beats RP on five of the six classifiers with paired McNemar $p_{\text{Holm}}<10^{-20}$. This is not a contradiction: the two encodings make different choices about what they discard. MTF discards within-bin amplitude but keeps quantile-ordered transition statistics, which is the right representation for non-instance-based classifiers; RP keeps amplitude (lower MAE) but the MDS-based reconstruction adds neighbourhood-geometry noise that hurts classifiers more than it hurts pointwise distance. The convergence at the top of the ranking and the inversion at the bottom together imply that, within the operating regime tested, the lenses are neither redundant nor universally consistent: they are sensitive to different aspects of the reconstruction, and the practical recommendation must therefore name the downstream task. For a reconstruction-faithful augmentation pipeline whose downstream consumer is a sample-level model, $\text{GASF}\succ\text{GADF}\succ\text{RP}\succ\text{MTF}$ is the right ordering; for a pipeline whose downstream consumer is a non-instance-based classifier of activity classes, $\text{GASF}\succ\text{GADF}\succ\text{MTF}\succ\text{RP}$ is.

\subsection{Scope of Claims}
\label{sec:disc_scope}

The empirical scope of the conclusions is the full LOTO 3-fold protocol and the grouped subject-out 3-fold protocol on the smartwatch accelerometer stream of WISDM, with windows of $N=128$ at 20\,Hz and five activities ($N=8\,227$ pooled paired test windows in each protocol). Within this scope, the top reconstruction tier is stable on every lens and every sampling scheme: GASF is the near-original decoder and is ahead of GADF in the reconstruction metrics and in five of the six classifiers, while GADF is the strongest reconstruction for KNN under grouped subject-out validation. Per-fold accuracy standard deviations are bounded by $0.02$ under LOTO and by $0.07$ under grouped subject-out validation. The bottom of the ranking is lens-dependent: on the reconstruction lenses (pointwise and polynomial) the order completes to $\text{GASF}\succ\text{GADF}\succ\text{RP}\succ\text{MTF}$, while on the classifier lens it completes to $\text{GASF}\succ\text{GADF}\succ\text{MTF}\succ\text{RP}$ in five of the six classifiers we evaluate (Section~\ref{sec:disc_convergence}). GASF is therefore the most faithful pointwise decoder and empirically near-equivalent for the evaluated classification task, but we do not claim formal information preservation. GADF is the second-best restricted-branch decoder on the reconstruction metrics and the strongest reconstruction under KNN subject-out validation (the only classifier-and-sampling combination where a reconstruction strictly beats the original), a result consistent with a regularization hypothesis but not sufficient to prove denoising. RP is an amplitude-preserving but classifier-fragile decoder; and MTF, under the spectral decoder of Section~\ref{sec:mtf_ablation}, is a classifier-friendly but amplitude-coarse representative reconstruction. The practical recommendation must therefore name the downstream consumer rather than collapse the comparison into an unconditional verdict on any single encoding. The broader generative-augmentation claim is not addressed by these numbers: every decoder in this paper assumes that the image is the deterministic output of its encoder, with side information (min--max, quantisation interval, sign convention) stored at encoding time. A diffusion or GAN that synthesises an image without storing those values would have to either learn them implicitly or be paired with a calibration step before any of these decoders can produce a physically plausible signal.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have presented a controlled, symmetric evaluation of four image-based encodings -- RP, GASF, GADF, MTF -- as inverse mappings from image to triaxial time series. Three of the four reconstruction pipelines used in this evaluation are themselves new operational contributions of this work and, to the best of our knowledge, have no prior fully specified counterpart in the literature reviewed here: the signed-RP-aware decoder of Section~\ref{sec:rp_reconstruction_method}, which uses the signed Lu--Tong RP definition as an in-loop constraint that resolves the multidimensional-scaling reflection ambiguity left open by Hirata, Horai, and Aihara \cite{hirata2008reproduction}; the restricted-branch GADF pivot-row decoder of Section~\ref{sec:gadf_reconstruction_proposal}, which turns the previously stated but under-specified invertibility of GADF on restricted angular ranges into an explicit operational decoder with branch control, angular anchoring, fixed sign convention, min--max calibration, and empirical evaluation; and the Fiedler-vector spectral MTF decoder of Sections~\ref{sec:mtf_reconstruction_method} and \ref{sec:mtf_ablation}, which reconstructs a representative compatible quantile trajectory rather than a bijective inverse of MTF. The fourth pipeline, the GASF decoder, is the only one that has direct precedent in the literature \cite{huamin2020reconstruction} and is kept in the comparison as a tightly controlled prior-art baseline. The four encoders are exercised on the same dataset, the same window size ($N=128$), both the LOTO 3-fold protocol and a grouped subject-out 3-fold protocol ($N=8\,227$ pooled test windows in each), the same classifier panel, and the same set of pointwise, polynomial-trend, and classification metrics. A symmetric evaluation across all four encodings -- under two complete 3-fold cross-validations, one within-trial and one subject-out -- is what allows the comparison reported here to be read as a direct ranking rather than as a partial inventory.

Within the tested protocol, GASF is the most faithful pointwise decoder, with a global MAE of $0.0209$, a Pearson correlation of $0.99988 \pm 0.00020$, and a classification accuracy that matches the originals within $0.001$ absolute accuracy across the six classifiers on the 3-fold mean. The paired McNemar tests detect no GASF degradation in the evaluated classifiers, but this should be read as empirical near-equivalence for this task rather than as formal information preservation. The restricted-branch GADF pivot-row decoder is second-best on the pointwise and polynomial reconstruction metrics, and usually second on classification (Pearson $0.983 \pm 0.025$, polynomial cosine similarity $0.99550$, LOTO classification gap of $0.01$ to $0.10$ absolute accuracy), confirming that the diagonal failure observed in the original GAF paper does not preclude reconstructability from the full matrix once an angular anchor, a sign convention, a restricted branch, and scale metadata are fixed. The gap between the two Gramian variants is itself statistically separated: Steiger's z for dependent correlations \cite{Steiger1980} on the pooled 3-fold sample, with $(r_{\text{orig,GASF}}=0.99988,\;r_{\text{orig,GADF}}=0.98334,\;r_{\text{GASF,GADF}}=0.96790)$ over $N=8\,227$ windows, yields $z=478.3$, $p<10^{-300}$, so the GASF advantage in pointwise fidelity is not within the noise of the GADF decoder.

The bottom of the ranking is lens-dependent. RP is an amplitude-preserving decoder ($\text{MAE}=2.95\pm 3.23$, Pearson $0.747\pm 0.175$) limited by the sign-flip ambiguity and the $N{-}1$ adjacent-state shrinkage, which our pipeline compensates with a per-window mean augmentation. MTF, under the Fiedler-vector decoder of Section~\ref{sec:mtf_ablation}, is a classifier-friendly but amplitude-coarse decoder ($\text{MAE}=4.00\pm 6.31$, Pearson $0.017\pm 0.84$) whose Q=4 quantization is information-theoretically lower-bounded by the within-bin variance but whose transition structure is preserved exactly by the Markov representation. On the pointwise lens RP wins ($\text{Wilcoxon }r=-0.351$ in favor of RP-vs-MTF MAE), but on the classifier lens MTF wins in five of the six classifiers (paired McNemar $p_{\text{Holm}}<10^{-20}$ in SVM, Random Forest, LSTM and CNN; marginal in GRU; KNN is the single exception that goes RP$\succ$MTF). The two encodings therefore make different trade-offs about what they discard, and the practical recommendation must therefore name the downstream consumer rather than collapse the ranking into a single number.

\paragraph*{Operator-level comparison of pairwise structure.}
Before discussing the first-order amplitude expansion that the rest of this section relies on, it is useful to put signed RP and GADF in a common notation without claiming equality of their output spaces. Both can be written as pairwise functions of lifted scalar samples,
\[
M_{ij} \;=\; \omega\!\bigl(\xi(x_i),\,\xi(x_j)\bigr),
\]
where $\xi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}$ is a two-dimensional lift of the scalar sample and $\omega:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}$ is the pairwise operator associated with the encoding. The two methods differ in both the lift and the operator:
\begin{itemize}
  \item RP under the Lu--Tong convention uses the adjacent-state embedding \(\xi_{\text{RP}}(x_k)=(x_k,\,x_{k+1})\) and a signed Euclidean distance, \(\omega_{\text{RP}}(u,v)=\sigma(u,v)\lVert u-v\rVert_{2}\), where the sign is assigned \emph{externally} by the projection onto the reference direction \(v_{\star}=(1,1)^{\top}/\sqrt{2}\). With the thresholded sign rule used here, \(\sigma(u,v)\) is not guaranteed to satisfy \(\sigma(u,v)=-\sigma(v,u)\) for every pair, so the resulting matrix is directional but not necessarily antisymmetric.
  \item GADF uses the polar lift \(\xi_{\text{GADF}}(\tilde x_k)=(\tilde x_k,\sqrt{1-\tilde x_k^{2}})=(\cos\phi_k,\sin\phi_k)\) and the operator \(\omega_{\text{GADF}}(u,v)=\det[u\mid v]=u_{1}v_{2}-u_{2}v_{1}\), the signed area of the parallelogram spanned by the two lifted points, whose sign is \emph{intrinsic} to the operator.
\end{itemize}
The important consequence is therefore an analogy, not an identity. GADF lives in the $\binom{N}{2}$-dimensional space of antisymmetric zero-diagonal matrices by construction, because the determinant changes sign when its arguments are swapped. Signed RP has a zero diagonal and carries directional pairwise-distance information, but the Lu--Tong sign rule used in this paper does not force exact antisymmetry for nearly orthogonal displacements. The first-order expansion below quantifies why the two images can nevertheless look and behave similarly, while the three concrete advantages of GADF over RP (intrinsic sign, closed-form $O(N)$ inverse, no recurrence-rate threshold) follow from the determinant-based operator rather than from an exact shared matrix space.

\paragraph*{A structural reading of why RP and GADF look (and behave) so similarly.}
Beyond the empirical ranking, the proximity between RP and GADF deserves a structural reading: their visual resemblance and their adjacent positions in the four-lens ranking are not coincidental. GADF produces an antisymmetric zero-diagonal matrix, while signed RP produces a zero-diagonal directional distance matrix with related pairwise-difference content. For smooth signals, both reduce, in first order, to functions of the same pairwise-difference matrix $\{x_i-x_j\}_{i,j}$. The signed RP under the Lu--Tong embedding satisfies
\[
R_{ij} \;\approx\; \sigma(i,j)\,\sqrt{2}\,|x_i-x_j|,
\]
where $\sigma(i,j)$ is the external orientation flag computed from the projection of $s_i-s_j$ onto $v=(1,1)^\top/\sqrt{2}$. The GADF entry, expanded around a common normalized amplitude $\bar{x}$, satisfies
\[
G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ij} \;=\; \sin(\phi_i-\phi_j) \;\approx\; \frac{\tilde{x}_j-\tilde{x}_i}{\sqrt{1-\bar{x}^2}},
\]
because $d(\arccos x)/dx=-1/\sqrt{1-x^2}$. The sign of the GADF term is intrinsic to the trigonometric form rather than imposed externally. Thus, both expressions are linear in pairwise amplitude differences at first order and both are zero on the diagonal, but only GADF is strictly antisymmetric under the stated construction. What makes the restricted-branch GADF decoder closed-form is that it (i) makes the sign part of the encoding rather than a post-hoc convention, and (ii) restricts the angular range to $[\arccos(\alpha),\arccos(-\alpha)]$ with $\alpha=0.7$, a near-maximal symmetric choice below $1/\sqrt{2}$ that keeps every $\sin(\phi_i-\phi_j)$ inside the principal branch of $\arcsin$. In other words, GADF and signed RP are structurally adjacent parametrizations of directional pairwise differences: GADF is the parametrization that makes the inverse map explicit under the branch restriction, RP is the one that preserves adjacent-state recurrence geometry but pays for it with the MDS sign-flip step and the threshold $\rho$. This reading clarifies why GADF is the natural decoder to ``rescue'' from the RP family --- they are closer to each other than to either GASF or MTF, and the restricted-branch GADF decoder sidesteps precisely the orientation ambiguity that limits RP.

\paragraph*{Practical implication: \{GASF, GADF\} suffices, RP becomes redundant for reconstruction.}
The structural analogy above has a direct consequence for protocol design. The visual resemblance between the RP and GADF images is not a coincidence of pixel statistics --- it follows from the first-order relations $R_{ij}\propto \sigma(i,j)|x_i-x_j|$ and $G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ij}\propto (\tilde{x}_j-\tilde{x}_i)/\sqrt{1-\bar{x}^2}$ derived above, and the null-corrected regional-overlap diagnostic of Section~\ref{sec:disc_rp_gadf_overlap} confirms that matched RP/GADF windows share more spatial region structure than same-activity random pairings. Because GADF captures a closely related directional pairwise-difference structure with three concrete advantages --- (i) intrinsic sign through $\sin$, (ii) a closed-form pivot-row inverse, and (iii) no dependence on the recurrence-rate threshold $\rho$ --- the pair $\{\text{GASF}, \text{GADF}\}$ already covers the most reconstruction-relevant content that RP contributes in the controlled setting of this paper. GASF encodes the symmetric, amplitude-bearing part of the Gramian (diagonal $\cos(2\phi_i)$); GADF encodes the antisymmetric, difference-bearing part ($\sin(\phi_i-\phi_j)$); RP encodes a directional distance structure with additional sensitivity to the local derivative through the $(x_i,x_{i+1})$ pair embedding. Our empirical numbers confirm this analytical picture: RP is dominated by GADF on every metric we measured (pointwise, polynomial, classifier), so for the controlled reconstruction problem studied here we recommend the $\{\text{GASF}, \text{GADF}\}$ pair as the canonical reconstruction baseline and treat RP as a representation whose value lies in dynamical-systems analysis (recurrence quantification, dynamical invariants, line-structure statistics) rather than in invertible imaging. This recommendation does not extend to use cases where the RP threshold and adjacent-state embedding are themselves the object of study; it is specific to the question of using image encodings as a reversible substrate for generative augmentation.

These results invite three follow-ups. First, the robustness of each decoder under perturbations of the encoded image -- additive noise, JPEG/PNG re-quantization, partial occlusion -- is the natural test of the augmentation hypothesis and is the missing piece for a generative-augmentation claim. Second, the GADF pivot row is currently selected heuristically by minimum mean magnitude; a principled selection rule (or a learned anchor predictor), together with sweeps over \(\alpha\), anchor-error rates, image-noise levels, and true-vs-global-vs-predicted min--max metadata, could close part of the GADF--GASF gap without leaving the closed-form regime. Third, the regularizing effect we observe in GADF-KNN under grouped subject-out validation (the only configuration where a reconstruction strictly beats the original) deserves a controlled study of its own. The result is consistent with a low-pass hypothesis, but power spectral density, high-frequency energy ratios, and smoothed-original baselines are required before interpreting GADF as a denoising mechanism. We expect that fixing these three follow-ups in turn would clarify when reconstruction-faithful encodings can substitute for the raw signal in downstream pipelines and when they cannot.
