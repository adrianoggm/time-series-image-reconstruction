\begin{abstract}
Image-based encodings of time series -- Recurrence Plots (RP), Markov Transition Fields (MTF), and the summation (GASF) and difference (GADF) variants of Gramian Angular Fields -- make temporal structure available to image-domain models \cite{wang2015encoding,wang2015encoding2}. This work does not evaluate generative augmentation; it studies the prerequisite inverse problem: whether images produced by known encoders can be mapped back to triaxial accelerometer windows with controlled loss.

We contribute three operational decoders with, to the best of our knowledge, no prior fully specified counterpart: a signed-RP decoder that uses the Lu--Tong sign convention to resolve the multidimensional-scaling reflection ambiguity left open by Hirata, Horai, and Aihara \cite{hirata2008reproduction}; a closed-form, boundary-anchored GADF decoder that turns the previously stated but under-specified invertibility of GADF into an explicit algorithm (fixed $\arcsin$ branch, image-derived anchor, sign convention, scale calibration); and a Fiedler-vector spectral MTF decoder that recovers a representative quantile trajectory. A GASF diagonal inverse \cite{huamin2020reconstruction} closes the four-encoding comparison as a controlled prior-art baseline.

Evaluated under a unified four-lens protocol -- visual inspection, pointwise error, polynomial-trend similarity, and six-classifier original-trained robustness -- on WISDM windows ($N=128$) under both LOTO and grouped subject-out 3-fold cross-validation, GASF and GADF are both near-original: once its branch, anchor and sign convention are fixed, GADF shows no statistically detectable classification difference from GASF or the raw signal (paired McNemar $p_{\text{Holm}}=1.0$ across all six classifiers) and near-identical pointwise error (global MAE $0.0156$ vs $0.0202$). Because GADF dominates RP on all reconstruction-oriented metrics and on five of the six classifiers, we recommend $\{\text{GASF},\text{GADF}\}$ as the canonical reconstruction-oriented GAF baseline -- without claiming formal information preservation -- and reserve RP for recurrence-geometry and instance-based settings. RP and MTF are the lossy pair with a lens-dependent inversion (RP wins pointwise, MTF on most classifiers); the exception is KNN, where the tuned-$\rho$ RP reconstruction beats GADF, GASF and the raw signal (subject-out $+0.044$ accuracy). When the inverse map and the encoder-side metadata are explicitly controlled, image-based encodings thus support a near-original reconstruction -- a necessary first step toward, but not a proof of, unconstrained generative augmentation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Time series data play a crucial role in fields ranging from finance and healthcare to human activity recognition. Despite their importance, many applications are constrained by scarce labelled data, which has motivated interest in data augmentation and, more broadly, in alternative representations that can leverage advanced methods from adjacent domains --- most notably the rich domain of image processing.

A common approach is to convert time-series signals into image-based representations. Techniques such as Markov Transition Fields (MTF), Recurrence Plots (RP), and Gramian Angular Fields (GAF) transform a one-dimensional sequence into a two-dimensional matrix whose pixel \((i,j)\) encodes a relation between two time indices. For triaxial accelerometer windows, the three axes are encoded independently and then treated as image channels. This construction allows image analysis and generation models to operate on temporal structure that is often difficult to expose directly in the raw signal.

Parallel to these developments, diffusion models have gained prominence in image generation. Their ability to synthesize highly structured images motivates the use of image representations for time-series augmentation, but this paper does not train or evaluate a diffusion or GAN model. We focus on the necessary inverse problem that must be solved first: if an image is produced by a known time-series encoder, can it be reconstructed back into the signal domain with controlled loss?

Our approach addresses this inverse problem by proposing dedicated reconstruction strategies for RP, MTF, and GAF-based encodings, and by evaluating the completed decoders under a unified framework that includes visual inspection, reconstruction metrics, polynomial trend comparison, and downstream classification. The overall workflow is summarized in Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow_overview}. Experiments on the WISDM Smartphone and Smartwatch Activity and Biometrics Dataset \cite{misc_wisdm_smartphone_and_smartwatch_activity_and_biometrics_dataset__507} show that the evaluated encodings preserve different aspects of the original signals and therefore support different use cases.

A central narrative choice in this paper is to separate \emph{controlled reconstruction} from \emph{unconstrained generative augmentation}. The experiments decode images produced by known encoders and, when required, use metadata stored during encoding, such as min--max ranges. This setting is the necessary first test of whether an image representation contains enough information to return to the time domain. It is not yet a claim that arbitrary images produced by a generative model will be physically plausible or reconstructable without additional calibration.


The paper is organized around three questions: what information each image encoding preserves, how that information can be mapped back to the time domain, and whether the reconstructed signals remain useful for downstream activity recognition. The main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a common operator-level formulation \(M_{ij}=\omega(\xi(x_i),\xi(x_j))\) that, in the conclusion, lets us read signed RP and GADF as structurally adjacent parametrisations of directional pairwise differences --- GADF lying in the antisymmetric zero-diagonal matrix space by construction, signed RP approximating it without exact antisymmetry under the Lu--Tong sign rule;
    \item \textbf{a novel RP decoder} that is the first to exploit the signed Lu--Tong RP definition to resolve the orientation ambiguity left open by the Hirata--Horai--Aihara reconstruction \cite{hirata2008reproduction} --- the ablation of Section~\ref{sec:rp_ablation} shows this orientation check is the decisive component, cutting the pointwise MAE by $43\%$ --- together with a lightweight $N{-}1\!\to\!N$ re-augmentation whose exact form (per-window mean vs endpoint) we find immaterial;
    \item \textbf{a novel restricted-branch, boundary-row-anchored GADF decoder} that operationalises the earlier observation by Wang and Oates \cite{wang2015encoding} that GADF can admit an accurate inverse map under restricted scaling: the contribution is a fully specified closed-form decoder with an explicit $\arcsin$ branch condition, image-derived angular anchor, fixed sign convention, optional min--max calibration for physical-scale recovery, and empirical evaluation under the same protocol as RP, MTF, and GASF, so it should not be read as a generic inverse for unconstrained GADF images;
    \item \textbf{a novel Fiedler-vector MTF decoder}, for which we are not aware of a prior explicit MTF-to-time-series decoder: rows of the field are clustered into $Q$ bins, the bin transition matrix is recovered, and the bin ordering is fixed by the Fiedler vector of the corresponding graph Laplacian; because MTF is quantised and many-to-one, the output is a representative compatible quantile trajectory rather than a bijective inverse;
    \item a GASF inversion pipeline that extends the diagonal-inverse line of work \cite{huamin2020reconstruction} with explicit handling of normalisation and quantisation, kept in the comparison as a controlled prior-art baseline rather than as a novel decoder;
    \item an explicit account of the assumptions that make each inverse map exact, approximate, or heuristic, and the metadata that each one requires;
    \item an evaluation design that separates pointwise fidelity, trend-level similarity, and classification utility, so that good classification is not mistaken for accurate signal reconstruction;
    \item a fully comparable evaluation of all four encodings (RP, MTF, GASF, GADF) under the same protocol -- same fold, same six-classifier panel, same pointwise and polynomial-trend metrics, both LOTO and grouped subject-out 3-fold.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work and Encodings}
\label{sec:related_encodings}
This section fixes the notation and summarizes the encoding mechanisms needed for the inverse problem. We distinguish between the image construction rule, which is part of the prior literature, and the reconstruction rule, which is where this paper contributes or identifies an explicit gap. For clarity, GAF denotes the family, whereas GASF and GADF denote the summation and difference variants, respectively.

All four encodings can be written as operators \(\mathcal{T}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}\), but they preserve different kinds of information. RP encodes geometric recurrences in a phase-space embedding, GASF and GADF encode angular relations after polar normalization, and MTF encodes transition probabilities between quantised states. This distinction is important for the rest of the paper: a representation that is useful for classification is not necessarily pointwise invertible, and a representation that is highly invertible under controlled conditions may still require metadata that are difficult to obtain for generated images.

\subsection{Recurrence Plot}
\subsubsection{Image Construction}
Recurrence plots were introduced by Eckmann et al. \cite{eckmann1995recurrence} and are commonly interpreted through phase-space reconstruction ideas developed for nonlinear dynamical systems \cite{takens1981detecting,marwan2007recurrence}. A scalar time series can be embedded as delay vectors
\[
\mathbf{s}_t=(x_t,x_{t+\tau},\ldots,x_{t+(m-1)\tau})\in\mathbb{R}^m,
\]
where \(m\) is the embedding dimension and \(\tau\) is the delay. In this work, we use the first-order embedding \(m=2\), \(\tau=1\), so \(s_i=(x_i,x_{i+1})\). The classical recurrence matrix is then defined as
\begin{equation}
   \mathbf{R}_{i,j}(\epsilon) = \boldsymbol{\theta}(\epsilon - \|s_i - s_j\|), \quad i,j = 1,\ldots,N-1,
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon$ is a threshold that determines the neighborhood size, $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ is the Heaviside step function, and $\|\cdot\|$ denotes a norm. Choosing an appropriate norm is crucial for constructing a meaningful recurrence plot; the most commonly used norms are $L_1$, $L_2$, and $L_\infty$ \cite{lu2019robust}.

One limitation of this definition is that the distance from \(x_i\) to \(x_j\) equals that from \(x_j\) to \(x_i\), yielding a symmetric matrix with respect to the main diagonal. Consequently, the mapping is not bijective: a time series and its inverted version produce identical images, meaning that decoding the image would yield two possible time-series representations. To overcome this issue, we adopt the alternative definition proposed by Jianjie Lu and Kai‐Yu Tong \cite{lu2019robust}, which addresses the bijective image representation problem and is formulated as follows:

Consider a time series \( x \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times N} \), where \( x_j \) denotes the \( j \)-th element of \( x \). The states are then defined as
\[
s_j = \bigl(x_j,\, x_{j+1}\bigr), \quad \text{with } s_j \in \mathbb{R}^2,
\]
so that the recurrence plot (RP) is constructed as a matrix \( R \in \mathbb{R}^{(N-1) \times (N-1)} \).

A basic formulation of the RP is given by
\begin{equation}
   R_{m,n} = \| s_m - s_n \|, \quad m,n = 1,\ldots,N-1,
\end{equation}
where \(\|\cdot\|\) denotes a chosen norm (e.g., \(L_1\), \(L_2\), or \(L_\infty\)).

To incorporate directional information, we calculate a sign based on the differences between state vectors. Throughout this work we use the fixed reference direction \(v=(1,1)^{\top}/\sqrt{2}\). For off-diagonal entries, define
\[
\gamma_{mn}=\frac{(s_m-s_n)\cdot v}{\|s_m-s_n\|\,\|v\|}, \qquad m\ne n.
\]
The sign convention assigns a negative value only when the off-diagonal displacement vector forms an angle greater than \(3\pi/4\) with respect to \(v\):
\begin{equation}
   \text{sign}(m,n)=
\begin{cases}
  1,  & \text{if } m=n, \\[0.5ex]
  -1, & \text{if } m\ne n \text{ and } \gamma_{mn}<\cos\left(\dfrac{3\pi}{4}\right), \\[1ex]
  1,  & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where \(\|v\|=1\). The diagonal convention avoids the undefined ratio at \(m=n\); since \(R_{m,m}=0\) after multiplication by the distance, the chosen diagonal sign has no numerical effect. This convention is used consistently during both RP encoding and the reconstruction-orientation check.

Finally, the recurrence plot is redefined as
\begin{equation}
   R_{m,n} = \text{sign}(m,n) \cdot \| s_m - s_n \|,
   \label{eq:FormulaRP}
\end{equation}
which is the definition we use to encode the RP as an image after normalizing the values and mapping them to RGB.

    \centering

    \caption{Example of a Recurrence Plot.}
\end{figure}
The adjacent-state construction produces \(N-1\) embedded states rather than \(N\) scalar samples. The information loss is not merely a count mismatch, since the overlapping states still contain all samples in principle; it arises because the RP stores pairwise distances or thresholded recurrences, which are invariant to transformations such as translation, rotation, reflection, and, depending on normalization, scale. The signed Lu--Tong convention used here adds an orientation cue, but it does not make the RP representation fully bijective.

\subsubsection{Time Series Reconstruction}
There is no direct inverse transformation for converting time series into recurrence plots according to the literature. However, several approaches have been proposed over time, primarily because this is one of the oldest methods for reconstruction. Notable contributions include those by M. Thiel \cite{THIEL2004343} and Tanio et al. \cite{TANIO20092031}. In particular, we highlight the decoding method by Hirata, Horai, and Aihara \cite{hirata2008reproduction}, which, as shown later, is adapted here to our signed RP definition (see Equation \ref{eq:FormulaRP}). This method applies to both continuous and binary recurrence plots.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Starting from a Recurrence Plot:}
    Let \(z(i)\) denote the state at time \(i\) (with \(1 \leq i \leq n\)). Define a function \(c\) that takes two states and indicates whether they are sufficiently close; it returns 1 if they are close and 0 otherwise, according to a predefined threshold. Thus, the recurrence plot can be represented as a binary cost matrix:
    \[
    \{(i,j) : c(z_i,z_j)=1,\quad i,j = 1,2,\dots,n\}.
    \]

    \item \textbf{Graph Construction:}
    Construct a graph in which an edge is drawn between nodes \(i\) and \(j\) if \(c(z_i,z_j)=1\).

    \item \textbf{Edge Weight Assignment:}
    For each state \(z_i\), define the set
    \[
    G_i = \{j : c(z_i,z_j)=1\},
    \]
    which represents the indices of time where state \(z_i\) is close to other states. Then, assign a weight to each edge between \(i\) and \(j\) as follows:
    \[
    w(i,j)= 1-\frac{|G_i \cap G_j|}{|G_i \cup G_j|}.
    \]

    \item \textbf{Shortest Path Calculation:}
    Compute the shortest path distances in the graph using Dijkstra's algorithm \cite{cherkassky1996shortest}. For larger graphs, an alternative method for finding the shortest path can be employed.

    \item \textbf{Multidimensional Scaling:}
    Finally, apply a multidimensional scaling technique that orders the set of points using the component corresponding to the largest eigenvalue. We use \emph{classical} (Torgerson) MDS, i.e.\ the closed-form double-centering eigendecomposition of $-\tfrac{1}{2}J D^{2} J$ with $J=I-\tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}$, rather than the SMACOF iterative optimiser. For a Euclidean-compatible distance matrix, this is the exact least-squares embedding, rather than an iterative approximation, and it removes the stochastic dependence on initialization that an iterative SMACOF solver would introduce.
\end{enumerate}

Although this graph-based method can recover a consistent state ordering, it has a notable drawback, also discussed in \cite{hirata2008reproduction}: multidimensional scaling may return a mirror-symmetric reconstruction. In our WISDM experiments, this ambiguity was observed before applying the signed-RP orientation check described below; therefore, the observed ambiguity should be understood as an empirical diagnostic of this implementation rather than as a parameter from the cited method.
\subsection{Gramian Angular Field}
\subsubsection{Image Construction}
\label{ConstruccionGAF}
Introduced by Wang, Z. and Oates, T. in \textit{Imaging Time-Series to Improve Classification and Imputation} \cite{wang2015encoding}, the Gramian Angular Field (GAF) technique represents time series values in polar coordinates. To do so, it is essential to rescale the time series values to an appropriate interval either to \([-1,1]\) to map to angles in \([0,\pi]\), or to \([0,1]\) to map to angles in \([0,\pi/2]\).

Once the time series is rescaled, the encoding is defined using the arccosine and the radius. Specifically, for the interval \([0,\pi]\) the definitions are:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{cases}
    \phi = \arccos(\tilde{x}_i), \quad -1 \leq \tilde{x}_i \leq 1,\quad \tilde{x}_i \in \tilde{X}, \\
    r = \frac{t_i}{N}, \quad t_i \in \mathbb{N},
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
and for the interval \([0,\pi/2]\) they become:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{cases}
    \phi = \arccos(\tilde{x}_i), \quad 0 \leq \tilde{x}_i \leq 1,\quad \tilde{x}_i \in \tilde{X}, \\
    r = \frac{t_i}{N}, \quad t_i \in \mathbb{N}.
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}

According to Wang and Oates \cite{wang2015encoding}, the polar transformation preserves temporal ordering through the radius and maps normalized amplitudes to angles through a monotonic arccosine branch. This does not mean that every GAF matrix has the same inverse properties. GASF and GADF use different pairwise angular operators, so the reconstruction argument must be derived separately for each case: GASF exposes amplitude information on the diagonal, whereas GADF stores only relative angular differences and has a zero diagonal.

Finally, two approaches to obtain a GAF are defined:
\begin{align}
G^{\mathrm{S}}_{ij} &= \cos(\phi_i + \phi_j) \nonumber \\
&= \tilde{x}_i\tilde{x}_j - \sqrt{1-\tilde{x}_i^2}\sqrt{1-\tilde{x}_j^2}, \label{eq:gasf_indexed}\\
G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ij} &= \sin(\phi_i - \phi_j) \nonumber \\
&= \sqrt{1-\tilde{x}_i^2}\tilde{x}_j - \tilde{x}_i\sqrt{1-\tilde{x}_j^2}. \label{eq:gadf_indexed}
\end{align}

    \centering

    \caption{Example of a Gramian Angular Field.}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Time Series Reconstruction}
\label{ReconstruccionGAF}
The two GAF variants require different reconstruction arguments. GASF has a known diagonal-based inverse under the appropriate normalization range, whereas GADF cannot be treated by simply reusing the same diagonal argument. We therefore separate the current GASF baseline from the GADF off-diagonal reconstruction developed below.

\paragraph{GASF reconstruction.}
In the original GAF paper \cite{wang2015encoding}, the possibility of recovering the original time series is connected to the structure of the Gramian representation. In subsequent literature, including a later paper by the same authors \cite{wang2015encoding2}, the practical reconstruction route is discussed through the Gramian Angular Summation Field (GASF) main diagonal.

For GASF inversion, we use the diagonal argument discussed by Huamin, Tao, and Qiuqun in \textit{Reconstruction of Time Series with Missing Values Using a 2D Representation Based Denoising Autoencoder} \cite{huamin2020reconstruction}, because it states the normalization conditions under which the diagonal can be used for reconstruction. Under those conditions, the normalized time series can be reconstructed by observing the main diagonal of a GASF. Specifically, since the diagonal elements satisfy
\[
G^{\mathrm{S}}_{ii} = \cos(2\phi_i),
\]
one can recover the normalized sample using the half-angle identity:
\[
\tilde{x}_i = \cos \phi_i = \sqrt{\frac{G^{\mathrm{S}}_{ii} + 1}{2}}, \quad \phi_i \in \left[0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right].
\]

It is important to note that the main-diagonal inversion is not unique for the interval \([-1,1]\), which maps to angles in \([0,\pi]\). In that case,
\[
G^{\mathrm{S}}_{ii}=\cos(2\phi_i)=2\tilde{x}_i^2-1,
\]
so the half-angle identity recovers only \(|\tilde{x}_i|\), not the sign of \(\tilde{x}_i\). This limitation applies to the diagonal-only inversion rule used here; it does not constitute a proof that no reconstruction could be obtained from the full GASF matrix or from additional side information.

Wang and Oates state that the GADF of $[0,1]$-rescaled data has an accurate inverse map \cite{wang2015encoding}. However, their reconstruction discussion develops the practical inverse through the GASF diagonal and does not provide an explicit GADF decoding algorithm. In particular, a GADF decoder must resolve the branch of $\arcsin$, fix the otherwise free global angular offset implied by the difference-only form $\sin(\phi_i-\phi_j)$, keep the sign convention fixed, and map the recovered angular trajectory back to physical units. Reconstruction via the main diagonal is not feasible since the angular differences yield
\[
G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ii} = \sin(\phi_i - \phi_i) = 0,
\]
regardless of whether the time series is scaled to \([-1,1]\) with angles in \([0,\pi]\) or to \([0,1]\) with angles in \([0,\pi/2]\). The method proposed later in Section~\ref{sec:gadf_reconstruction_proposal} therefore uses off-diagonal angular differences rather than the diagonal.


\subsection{Markov Transition Field}
\label{sec:mtf_definition}
\subsubsection{Image Construction}
Introduced by Wang and Oates in \textit{Imaging Time-Series to Improve Classification and Imputation} \cite{wang2015encoding}, the Markov Transition Field (MTF) is probabilistic rather than geometric or trigonometric. Given a time series \(X\), the values are divided into \(Q\) quantile bins and each sample is assigned a discrete state \(q_t\in\{1,\ldots,Q\}\). The first-order Markov transition matrix is estimated as
\[
W_{p,q}=P(q_{t+1}=q\mid q_t=p)=\frac{\#\{t:q_t=p,\ q_{t+1}=q\}}{\#\{t:q_t=p\}},
\]
with rows normalized to sum to one when the source state is observed.

The MTF expands this \(Q\times Q\) matrix back to an \(N\times N\) image by assigning each pixel the transition probability associated with the states of its two time indices:
\begin{equation}
M_{a,b}=W_{q_a,q_b},\qquad a,b=1,\ldots,N.
\end{equation}
Thus, each pixel stores a transition statistic between quantile states rather than a direct distance or angular relation between amplitudes. This explains why MTF can be robust for classification while being weak for pointwise reconstruction: all samples inside the same quantile share the same reconstructed representative value.

    \centering
    \encodingexampleplot{images/0x0mtf.png}
    \caption{Example of a Markov Transition Field.}
\end{figure}
\subsubsection{Time Series Reconstruction }
In the literature, there is no documented method for estimating the original time series from a Markov Transition Field (MTF) matrix. Some articles use Markov Random Fields for image estimation and reconstruction, applying them as image filters to improve reconstructed image quality \cite{kopec1994document,kliewer2006iterative}. However, those methods address image restoration rather than inverse MTF decoding.




\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem_formulation}
Let \(x=(x_1,\ldots,x_N)\) denote one axis of a triaxial accelerometer window, with \(N=128\) in all experiments. For an encoding method \(m\), the image construction is written as
\[
\mathcal{E}_m:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N_m\times N_m\times C},
\]
where \(C\) denotes the number of image channels after the matrix representation is normalized and stored. In our setting, \(N_m=N\) for GASF, GADF, and MTF, while the modified RP representation internally uses adjacent states and therefore has \(N_m=N-1\) before the reconstruction is resampled back to \(N\). The inverse problem studied in this paper is to construct a decoder
\[
\mathcal{D}_m:\mathbb{R}^{N_m\times N_m\times C}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
\]
such that \(\hat{x}=\mathcal{D}_m(\mathcal{E}_m(x))\) is close to the original signal under pointwise, trend-level, and downstream classification criteria. The same notation is used for each accelerometer axis; the triaxial reconstruction is obtained by applying the decoder independently to the three encoded channels and then regrouping the axes into a single window.

This formulation separates three sources of difficulty. First, some encodings introduce invariances or many-to-one summaries by construction. In RP, the adjacent-state embedding produces \(N-1\) overlapping two-dimensional states that still contain all samples in principle, but the stored pairwise distances or recurrences are invariant to transformations such as translation, rotation, reflection, and, depending on normalization, scale; the decoder then also has to map the recovered \(N-1\) state sequence back to \(N\) samples. Second, some encodings introduce branch or orientation ambiguities, as in GASF diagonal inversion, GADF sine-difference inversion, and multidimensional scaling for RP. Third, image storage and normalization introduce practical quantisation and rescaling errors. The proposed decoders therefore report their assumptions explicitly: normalization range, required metadata, orientation convention, and whether the reconstruction is exact, approximate, or heuristic.

The rest of the paper follows this formulation. Section~\ref{sec:proposed_methods} describes the decoders, including the proposed GADF boundary-row-anchored reconstruction. Section~\ref{sec:experimental_protocol} defines the data and metrics, Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the complete empirical evidence for all four encodings, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets the trade-offs among encodings. This organization is intentional: methods may be mathematically defined before all empirical evidence is available, but comparative claims are restricted to the decoders tested under the same protocol.

\section{Proposed Reconstruction Methods}
\label{sec:proposed_methods}
This section describes the reconstruction strategy associated with each encoding. Each method is presented as an inverse map with explicit assumptions, so that later experiments can be read as tests of these assumptions rather than as isolated implementation results.

\subsection{Shared normalisation, storage, and calibration protocol}
\label{sec:shared_protocol}
All four encoders and decoders operate on a per-window, per-axis normalised signal, and the mapping back to physical units is a single external stage shared by every method (the normalisation and denormalisation blocks in Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow_overview}). Writing $\mathcal{N}$ for the per-window normaliser and $\mathcal{E}_m,\mathcal{D}_m$ for the encoder and decoder of method $m$, the pipeline is
\[
\tilde{x}=\mathcal{N}(x),\qquad \hat{\tilde{x}}=\mathcal{D}_m\!\big(\mathcal{E}_m(\tilde{x})\big),
\]
where the encoded matrix is stored and re-read as an 8-bit PNG between $\mathcal{E}_m$ and $\mathcal{D}_m$. \textbf{Unless otherwise stated, every decoder returns its reconstruction in the normalised domain} $\hat{\tilde{x}}$; the per-axis minimum and maximum $(x_{\min},x_{\max})$ recorded at encoding time are used \emph{only} when the normalised series must be mapped back to the physical accelerometer units,
\[
\hat{x}=\mathcal{N}^{-1}(\hat{\tilde{x}};\,x_{\min},x_{\max}).
\]
Metadata dependence is therefore conditional on the desired output domain: no per-window amplitude metadata are needed for normalised reconstruction, whereas physical-scale reconstruction requires the original scale calibration. The \emph{normalisation range}, by contrast, is part of each encoder--decoder definition and is not interchangeable: the diagonal GASF inverse assumes the $[0,1]$ range (angles in $[0,\pi/2]$), the restricted-branch GADF inverse assumes $[-\alpha,\alpha]$ with $\alpha<1/\sqrt{2}$, MTF discretises the normalised range into $Q$ quantile bins, and the signed-RP orientation check requires the encoder and decoder to share the same signed-RP normalisation convention. Each subsection below therefore states its normalisation range explicitly, returns a normalised reconstruction, and defers the optional physical-scale step to the common $\mathcal{N}^{-1}$ above.

\subsection{Recurrence Plot}
\label{sec:rp_reconstruction_method}
The Recurrence Plot decoder proposed below is a \emph{new} contribution of this work; the closest precedent in the literature is the unsigned reconstruction of Hirata, Horai, and Aihara \cite{hirata2008reproduction}, which terminates at the multidimensional-scaling step and leaves both the orientation ambiguity (a global sign flip after MDS) and the $N\rightarrow N-1$ shrinkage of the adjacent-state embedding unresolved. We extend that pipeline in two ways that, taken together, turn it into a usable inverse map for the signed RP variant studied in this paper: we exploit the \emph{signed} Lu--Tong RP definition (Equation~\ref{eq:FormulaRP}) to disambiguate the MDS reflection by an empirical orientation check, and we re-augment the reconstructed sequence from $N-1$ adjacent-state points back to $N$ samples through a per-window mean appended before cubic-spline resampling. The signed-RP orientation check is, to the best of our knowledge, the first use of the signed RP convention as a constraint inside a reconstruction decoder rather than as an encoder-side choice. The decoder is therefore presented as a controlled inverse map: the encoder--decoder pair is well defined on the protocol of this paper, but neither step is universally invertible.

Multidimensional scaling can recover a compatible one-dimensional geometry, but only up to translation, scale, and reflection; therefore, the decoder must explicitly restore the missing sample and select the orientation most consistent with the signed RP convention. Concretely:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Step 1:} Define a recurrence-rate parameter \(\rho=0.60\) (selected by the sensitivity analysis of Figure~\ref{fig:rp_rho_sensitivity}; the initial value was \(0.75\)). Let \(d_{ij}=\|z_i-z_j\|_2\) be the Euclidean distance between states and let \(\tau\) be the empirical \(\rho\)-quantile of the off-diagonal distances. We set \(c_{ij}=1\) if \(d_{ij}\leq\tau\), and \(c_{ij}=0\) otherwise. Thus, the 60\% closest state pairs are considered recurrent.
    \item \textbf{Step 2:} Construct a graph from the binary matrix by establishing an edge between nodes \(i\) and \(j\) if \(c_{ij} = 1\); no edge is created if \(c_{ij} = 0\).
    \item \textbf{Step 3:} Assign a weight to each edge. Define
    \[
    G_i = \{ j \mid c(z_i, z_j) = 1 \},
    \]
    which represents the set of time indices where state \(z_i\) is close to other states. Then, for each edge between \(i\) and \(j\), set the weight as
    \[
    w(i, j) = 1 - \frac{|G_i \cap G_j|}{|G_i \cup G_j|}.
    \]
    \item \textbf{Step 4:} Compute the shortest path distances in the graph using Dijkstra's algorithm, based on the weights assigned.
    \item \textbf{Step 5:} Apply a multidimensional scaling method to the set of points, ordering them by the component corresponding to the largest eigenvalue.
    \item \textbf{Step 6:} Once multidimensional scaling has produced one of the two mirror-symmetric solutions, we use the signed RP convention in Equation~\ref{eq:FormulaRP} to select the orientation that best matches the encoded image. This is an empirical orientation check under the fixed reference direction \(v=(1,1)^\top/\sqrt{2}\), not a proof of global bijectivity for the RGB-normalized representation. The process is as follows:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Step 6.1:} Append the mean value of the reconstructed series. This adjustment is necessary because the reconstructed series contains \(N-1\) elements, where \(N\) is the length of the original series, as a consequence of the new RP definition.
        \item \textbf{Step 6.2 (Optional):} Rescale the reconstructed time series using a predefined lookup table, but only if the range values of the original time series are available.
        \item \textbf{Step 6.3:} Generate two potential reconstructions: \(rec_1\) as the direct reconstruction and \(rec_2 = rec_1 \times (-1)\). For each reconstruction, compute the associated signed RP distance matrix using Equation~\ref{eq:FormulaRP} and the same normalization convention used for the original RP image.
        \item \textbf{Step 6.4:} Compare the signed RP matrices of both reconstructions with the decoded original signed RP matrix by calculating the Euclidean distance between them; this comparison is performed before any binary thresholding.
        \item \textbf{Step 6.5:} Select the reconstruction with the smaller Euclidean distance as the candidate most consistent with the encoded signed RP matrix.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Step 7:} Perform cubic-spline interpolation on the reconstructed points and resample the interpolated function to restore the original time-series length of \(N\) values, compensating for the initial reduction to \(N-1\) points.
\end{itemize}
This approach does not make RP fully invertible; instead, it makes the main sources of ambiguity explicit. The signed-RP comparison reduces the reflection ambiguity introduced by multidimensional scaling, while interpolation restores the original length \(N\) from the \(N-1\) adjacent-state representation.


\subsection{Gramian Angular Summation Field Reconstruction}
\label{sec:gasf_reconstruction_method}
Unlike the other three decoders in this section, the GASF inverse is \emph{not} a new contribution of this work: it follows the diagonal-inverse strategy used by Huamin et al.~\cite{huamin2020reconstruction} in the 2-D representation-based denoising autoencoder line of work. We re-derive it here in the notation of the rest of the paper and re-implement it inside the same pipeline used by the other three decoders, so the GASF numbers reported later can be read as a tightly controlled prior-art baseline against which the three novel decoders (RP, GADF, MTF) are compared. The contribution of this subsection is therefore the explicit normalisation and quantisation handling that make the inverse stable when the GASF matrix is read back from an 8-bit PNG, not the inverse map itself.

The GASF decoder is the most direct inverse considered in the completed experiments because its main diagonal contains a sample-wise angular constraint. Under the branch used in this work, the encoded values are first interpreted through
\[
G^{\mathrm{S}}_{ii}=\cos(2\phi_i),
\]
and the angle is recovered as
\[
\hat{\phi}_i=\frac{1}{2}\arccos\left(G^{\mathrm{S}}_{ii}\right).
\]
The normalized signal is then obtained by
\[
\hat{\tilde{x}}_i=\cos(\hat{\phi}_i).
\]
This rule is exact only when the normalization and angular branch are consistent with the encoder. If the full interval \([-1,1]\) is used without additional sign information, the diagonal determines \(|\tilde{x}_i|\) rather than \(\tilde{x}_i\) itself. Therefore, the high numerical accuracy reported for GASF should be read as the result of a controlled normalization protocol, not as an unconditional inverse for every possible GAF image.

The recovered $\hat{\tilde{x}}_i=\cos(\hat{\phi}_i)$ is the normalised reconstruction; when physical accelerometer units are wanted, it is passed through the common calibration $\mathcal{N}^{-1}$ of Section~\ref{sec:shared_protocol}. The normalised shape is thus recovered from the diagonal alone, and the stored min--max is needed only for the optional physical-scale step --- which is also why a generated GASF image would require plausible scale metadata only if physical units, rather than normalised shape, are required.


\subsection{Restricted-Branch Gramian Angular Difference Field Reconstruction}
\label{sec:gadf_reconstruction_proposal}
We now describe a restricted-branch GADF decoder. Wang and Oates note that GADF can admit an accurate inverse map when the data are rescaled to \([0,1]\) \cite{wang2015encoding}, but their reconstruction discussion operationalises the inverse through the GASF diagonal and does not specify a GADF decoding algorithm. In particular, GADF stores \(\sin(\phi_i-\phi_j)\), so an operational inverse must choose an \(\arcsin\) branch, fix the otherwise free global angular offset, keep a sign convention fixed, and map the recovered angular trajectory back to physical units. The contribution here is therefore not the abstract observation that GADF may be invertible under a restricted angular range, but a fully specified operational decoder: a closed-form boundary-row-anchored reconstruction with an explicit branch condition, an image-derived angular anchor, a fixed sign convention, optional min--max calibration for physical-scale recovery, and empirical evaluation under the same protocol as RP, MTF, and GASF. It should not be read as a generic inverse for every standard GADF image on the full \([-1,1]\) range. The novelty lies in two coupled design choices: (i) we shrink the normalised amplitude range to $[-\alpha,\alpha]$ with $\alpha=0.7$ so that \emph{every} pairwise angular difference is forced inside the principal branch of $\arcsin$, which is what turns the inverse from an iterative optimisation into a one-pass closed-form computation, and (ii) we anchor the otherwise-relative angular trajectory by reading the angular boundary directly off the extreme row of the GADF image (rather than from an external time-domain index), so that the normalised trajectory is recovered from the image alone --- the stored $(x_{\min},x_{\max})$ being needed only for optional physical-scale recovery.

GADF requires a reconstruction strategy different from the GASF diagonal inverse. The diagonal of \(G^{\mathrm{D}}\) is identically zero, but the full matrix stores relative angular information through \(G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ij}=\sin(\phi_i-\phi_j)\). The proposed decoder uses this off-diagonal structure and turns a row of the GADF matrix into a set of recoverable angular differences.

\subsubsection{Restricted scaling for branch uniqueness}
The general GADF inverse is not unique without additional assumptions because \(\sin(\theta)=\sin(\pi-\theta)\). The key design decision is therefore to restrict the normalized amplitude range before encoding. This sacrifices part of the nominal \([-1,1]\) GAF range, but it keeps all pairwise angular differences inside the principal branch of \(\arcsin\), which is what makes a closed-form decoder possible. The input series is scaled as
\[
\bar{x}_t = 1.4\frac{x_t-x_{\min}}{x_{\max}-x_{\min}}-0.7, \qquad \bar{x}_t\in[-\alpha,\alpha],\quad \alpha=0.7.
\]
The corresponding angles satisfy
\[
\phi_t=\arccos(\bar{x}_t)\in[\arccos(\alpha),\arccos(-\alpha)]\approx[0.795,2.346].
\]
The value \(\alpha=0.7\) is not an arbitrary hyperparameter. It is chosen as a near-maximal symmetric amplitude range that preserves the injectivity of the inverse-sine step. For the closed-form GADF inverse to be unique, every angular difference must remain inside the principal branch of \(\arcsin\):
\[
|\phi_i-\phi_j|<\frac{\pi}{2}.
\]
The worst-case angular spread is
\[
\arccos(-\alpha)-\arccos(\alpha).
\]
Using \(\arccos(-\alpha)=\pi-\arccos(\alpha)\), the uniqueness condition becomes
\[
\pi-2\arccos(\alpha)<\frac{\pi}{2},
\]
or equivalently
\[
\alpha<\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\approx0.7071.
\]
We therefore set \(\alpha=0.7\), close to the largest admissible symmetric range while leaving a small numerical margin:
\[
|\phi_i-\phi_j|\leq \arccos(-0.7)-\arccos(0.7)\approx 1.551 < \frac{\pi}{2}.
\]
This keeps every angular difference within the principal branch of \(\arcsin\). Under this restriction,
\[
\arcsin\left(G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ij}\right)=\phi_i-\phi_j.
\]
Without the restriction \(\alpha<1/\sqrt{2}\), the same value \(\sin(\phi_i-\phi_j)\) could correspond to multiple angular-difference configurations, and the GADF matrix would no longer determine the angular differences uniquely. The branch restriction gives a single angular reconstruction under the controlled protocol, once the absolute angular anchor is also fixed (the min--max metadata is not needed for this normalised reconstruction, only for the optional physical-scale recovery). The scaling ablation of Section~\ref{sec:gadf_ablation} sweeps \(\alpha\), separates floating-point reconstruction error (which is exact) from 8-bit PNG quantisation error, and finds a $0\%$ anchor-misidentification rate on WISDM.

\subsubsection{Relative angular reconstruction from a pivot row}
For a pivot row \(p\), the GADF entries provide
\[
G^{\mathrm{D}}_{pj}=\sin(\phi_p-\phi_j),
\]
and, by the branch condition above,
\[
\phi_j=\phi_p-\arcsin\left(G^{\mathrm{D}}_{pj}\right),\qquad j=1,\ldots,N.
\]
Thus a pivot row determines the entire angular trajectory only up to a global offset; the absolute offset is fixed separately by the boundary (extreme-mean) anchor described below. The pivot is chosen from the image itself rather than from an external time-domain index, because a stored or generated GADF image may not preserve the original contextual information. We select the pivot row as
\[
p=\arg\min_i \left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ij}\right|,
\]
which chooses the row whose average signed angular difference is closest to zero. Because \(G^{\mathrm{D}}\) is anti-symmetric, this row is typically close to the angular center of the series and reduces the maximum magnitude of the recovered differences.

\subsubsection{Absolute angular anchor}
The pivot row reconstructs angles relative to \(\phi_p\), so one absolute angular anchor is still required --- but this anchor is supplied by the \emph{known} normalisation range, not by the stored min--max. Because the normalisation is performed per window, the encoded normalised signal necessarily reaches the two boundary values \(+\alpha\) and \(-\alpha\) unless the window is constant, so the extreme samples sit at fixed, \emph{a priori} known angular boundaries:
\[
\phi_{\max}=\arccos(\alpha), \qquad \phi_{\min}=\arccos(-\alpha).
\]
Operationally, the boundary row is identified from the image as the extreme row
\[
e=\arg\max_i \left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ij}\right|.
\]
The sign of the mean row indicates which boundary is represented: a positive mean corresponds to the large-angle boundary \(\phi_e=\arccos(-\alpha)=\phi_{\min}\), whereas a negative mean corresponds to the small-angle boundary \(\phi_e=\arccos(\alpha)=\phi_{\max}\).

This identification is exact in the noise-free restricted regime, because the row mean is a strictly monotonic function of the row angle. Writing \(\bar C=\tfrac1N\sum_j\cos\phi_j\) and \(\bar S=\tfrac1N\sum_j\sin\phi_j\),
\[
\frac1N\sum_{j=1}^{N}G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ij}=\bar C\sin\phi_i-\bar S\cos\phi_i=R\,\sin(\phi_i-\delta),\qquad R=\sqrt{\bar C^2+\bar S^2},\ \ \delta=\operatorname{atan2}(\bar S,\bar C),
\]
whose derivative \(R\cos(\phi_i-\delta)\) is strictly positive over the whole admissible range. Indeed every \(\phi_j\in[\arccos\alpha,\arccos(-\alpha)]\), and \(\delta\) is the argument of a convex combination of the unit vectors \((\cos\phi_j,\sin\phi_j)\), so \(\delta\) lies in that same interval and \(\phi_i-\delta\in[-(\arccos(-\alpha)-\arccos\alpha),\,\arccos(-\alpha)-\arccos\alpha]\). For \(\alpha=0.7\) this spread is \(\approx 1.551<\pi/2\) --- the same margin that keeps the \(\arcsin\) branch unique --- hence \(\cos(\phi_i-\delta)>0\) throughout and the row mean is strictly increasing in \(\phi_i\). Its extreme value is therefore attained exactly at the boundary sample \((\pm\alpha)\) that the per-window normalization is guaranteed to produce, and the sign of that extreme selects the correct boundary. The \(\alpha=0.7\) restriction thus secures branch uniqueness and unambiguous anchor identification at once.

Once one of these boundary rows is identified, the rest of the angular trajectory follows directly from
\[
\phi_j=\phi_e-\arcsin\left(G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ej}\right),\qquad j=1,\ldots,N,
\]
under the restricted-branch condition, and the pivot angle follows as the special case
\[
\phi_p=\phi_e-\arcsin\left(G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ep}\right).
\]
The reconstruction is therefore unique within the controlled protocol: the GADF supplies all pairwise angular differences, while the boundary value imposed by the per-window normalization fixes the otherwise free global angular offset.
Finally, the normalized series is recovered as
\[
\hat{\bar{x}}_j=\cos(\phi_j),\qquad j=1,\ldots,N.
\]
If the stored matrix uses the transposed GADF convention \(\sin(\phi_j-\phi_i)\), the signs in the two equations above are reversed; the implementation must therefore keep the convention in Equation~\ref{eq:gadf_indexed} fixed.

\subsubsection{Normalised output and limitations}
The GADF image, the fixed value of \(\alpha\), and the encoding sign convention are sufficient to recover the normalised trajectory \(\hat{\bar{x}}_j=\cos(\phi_j)\in[-\alpha,\alpha]\) directly from the image; the per-window minimum and maximum are \emph{not} required for this step. The calibration pair \((x_{\min},x_{\max})\) enters only through the common physical-scale map \(\mathcal{N}^{-1}\) of Section~\ref{sec:shared_protocol}, i.e.\ only when the reconstructed series must be expressed in the original accelerometer units; metadata dependence is thus conditional on the output domain. The method is therefore closed-form and linear in \(N\) after row statistics have been computed, but it is not universally invertible. It relies on the restricted scaling interval \([-\alpha,\alpha]\) with \(\alpha<1/\sqrt{2}\), a consistent GADF sign convention, and the presence or reliable identification of an angular boundary. Stating these requirements is essential for the paper's scope: the decoder is a controlled inverse map, not a guarantee that every visually plausible GADF image corresponds to a valid physical signal. A quantisation-specific caveat applies near the boundary rows: the entry between the two extreme samples reaches \(|G^{\mathrm{D}}|=\sin\!\big(\arccos(-\alpha)-\arccos\alpha\big)\approx 0.9998\), where \(\arcsin\) has derivative \(\approx 50\), so the \(1/255\) step of 8-bit PNG storage maps to an angular error of order \(0.2\,\)rad on the sample farthest from the anchor --- about an order of magnitude above the exact-arithmetic branch margin. The decoder is thus exact in real arithmetic but quantisation-limited near the boundary, which is the natural target of the image-perturbation robustness study left to future work. When these assumptions fail, a more general nonlinear least-squares reconstruction can be used:
\[
\min_{\phi}\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}\left(\sin(\phi_i-\phi_j)-G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ij}\right)^2,
\]
with one angle fixed to remove the global phase ambiguity and with smoothness or locality weights \(w_{ij}\) for robustness.

\subsection{Markov Transition Field Reconstruction}
\label{sec:mtf_reconstruction_method}
The MTF decoder described below is, to the best of our knowledge, not preceded by a documented explicit MTF-to-time-series decoder in the literature reviewed here. The decoder is not bijective: a Markov chain induces an equivalence class of time series with the same transition statistics, and no algorithm can distinguish elements of that class from the MTF image alone. What follows recovers a representative of that class by clustering the MTF rows into quantile bins and fixing the bin ordering through the Fiedler vector of the recovered transition graph. This spectral ordering step is motivated by the seriation literature, where the Fiedler vector can recover a latent linear order under appropriate structural conditions \cite{Atkins1998}; in our setting, the exact-order statement should therefore be read as an ideal birth--death-chain case, not as a universal guarantee for arbitrary MTF images.

The reconstruction proceeds in four steps:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Step 1: Bin recovery.} Cluster the $T$ rows of the MTF matrix $M\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}$ into $Q$ groups by farthest-point-initialised $k$-means on the row-normalised image. Under a clean MTF every sample in the same quantile bin has the same row pattern, so this recovers the bin label sequence $\{q_1,\ldots,q_T\}$ up to a bin permutation.

    \item \textbf{Step 2: Bin ordering via the Fiedler vector.} Estimate the bin transition matrix $W$ as the average of MTF entries inside every $(p,q)$ block, symmetrise it as $S=\tfrac{1}{2}(W+W^{\top})$, and order the bins by the Fiedler vector of the graph Laplacian $L=D-S$ (the eigenvector corresponding to the second-smallest eigenvalue). For an ideal birth--death Markov chain on $Q$ ordered states this recovers the linear amplitude order; for general chains it is a spectral seriation heuristic whose quality depends on how strongly the recovered transition graph reflects an underlying one-dimensional amplitude order.

    \item \textbf{Step 3: Amplitude assignment.} For every time index $i$ belonging to sorted bin $G_k$, assign the midpoint of the corresponding quantile interval as the reconstructed normalised value:
    \[
    \hat{x}_i=\frac{k-\tfrac{1}{2}}{Q}, \qquad k=1,\ldots,Q.
    \]

    \item \textbf{Step 4: Normalised output.} Steps 1--3 already return the reconstruction in the normalised domain (bin-midpoint values in $[0,1]$); recovery of the physical scale, when required, follows the common calibration $\mathcal{N}^{-1}$ of Section~\ref{sec:shared_protocol}.
\end{itemize}

The procedure does not verify the global sign of the series, because the MTF representation emphasises transition structure rather than signed amplitude order; a single sign-bit per axis (not stored here) would resolve the remaining mirror ambiguity. We use $Q=4$ bins throughout the reported experiments. The choice is defended both by a noise-of-estimator argument -- with $T=128$ samples and $Q$ bins there are $\sim (T-1)/Q^{2}$ transitions per cell of the empirical row-stochastic matrix $W$, so $Q=4$ admits $\sim 8$ transitions per cell while $Q=8$ drops to $\sim 2$ and many cells of $W$ become degenerate at $0$ or $1$ -- and empirically by the bin-count ablation in Section~\ref{sec:mtf_ablation}, where $Q=4$ is the value that simultaneously minimises the pointwise MAE, maximises the pointwise Pearson correlation, and maximises the amplitude-sensitive KNN accuracy (CNN being essentially flat across $Q$). The remaining classifiers (SVM, Random Forest, LSTM, GRU) vary by at most $0.012$ accuracy across the entire sweep, so the recommendation of $Q=4$ is dominated by the amplitude-sensitive subset rather than overridden by the others.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:experimental_protocol}
The experimental protocol is designed to answer three separate questions: whether the signal can be reconstructed point-by-point, whether its global trend is preserved, and whether the reconstructed window still supports activity recognition. These questions are intentionally separated because a representation may be poor for sample-level reconstruction while still retaining enough coarse structure for classification. This section fixes the dataset, segmentation, qualitative checks, metrics, and classifier protocol before the results are reported.

\subsection{Dataset and Segmentation}
The dataset used is the \textit{WISDM Smartphone and Smartwatch Activity and Biometrics Dataset} \cite{misc_wisdm_smartphone_and_smartwatch_activity_and_biometrics_dataset__507}. It contains data from 51 subjects, with accelerometer and gyroscope measurements collected from a smartwatch worn on the dominant hand and a mobile device kept in the pocket. The dataset includes 18 activities, each recorded for 3 minutes. We use the smartwatch accelerometer stream, which is sampled at 20\,Hz (i.e., one sample every 50\,ms).

For our experiments, we selected five common activities with nearly balanced sample counts. This subset keeps the classification task interpretable while avoiding a strongly imbalanced evaluation in which reconstruction effects could be masked by class-frequency effects. The chosen activities are:

We segmented the data into fixed non-overlapping windows (FNOW) of length \(N=128\), corresponding to 6.4\,seconds at 20\,Hz. The window length is fixed at a power of two because it is the de-facto standard for convolutional/recurrent backbones used in human-activity recognition, and because it keeps the GASF, GADF, and MTF images at \(128\times128\) pixels. For the modified RP formulation, adjacent states are still written as \(s_j=(x_j,x_{j+1})\), so a length-\(N\) window yields an \((N-1)\times(N-1)=127\times127\) image; the reconstruction pipeline then augments the recovered \(N-1\) samples with the per-window mean (step 6.1 of the Hirata--Horai--Aihara adaptation) so that the final signal matches the 128-sample window length used by the other encodings. With \(N_{\text{bins}}=4\), the MTF partition contains four equal-size groups of \(N/Q=32\) time indices, which exactly tiles the window with no leftover index.

By decomposing the X, Y, and Z dimensions into individual time series of size 128, we obtain 8\,172 windows per channel, resulting in a total of $8\,172 \times 3 = 24\,516$ one-dimensional reconstructions for the reconstruction metrics. For classification, however, the experimental unit is the full triaxial window: the three reconstructed axes are grouped back into a single sample, yielding 8\,172 labeled windows. The LOTO protocol assigns these to three folds of $\sim$2\,724 test windows each, so the pooled paired-sample size used by the inferential tests in Section~\ref{sec:statistical_protocol} is $N=8\,172$.

\paragraph{Windowing and grouping details.} Windowing is performed \emph{independently within each (subject, activity) segment}: inside one contiguous recording of a given activity by a given subject we take non-overlapping blocks of $N=128$ samples (stride $128$) and discard the trailing remainder shorter than $128$. No window therefore crosses a subject or an activity boundary, and every window carries an unambiguous single label and subject id. Dropping the per-segment remainders is what reduces the naive global-slide count to the $8\,172$ windows used here, and it removes the boundary-straddling windows that a global slide would otherwise mislabel. Two grouping protocols are then applied with \texttt{StratifiedGroupKFold}. In the subject-out protocol (LOSO) the grouping key is the subject id, so every window of a given subject falls entirely in one fold and no test subject is seen during training; this is the protocol that measures generalisation to unseen individuals. In the protocol we abbreviate LOTO, the grouping key is effectively unique per window, so the split reduces to a class-stratified partition at the window level: although no single window ever appears in both train and test, different --- and often temporally adjacent --- windows from the same subject and activity are distributed across the two sets. LOTO therefore quantifies robustness to reconstruction-induced distribution shift \emph{within} the data distribution rather than true leave-trials-out generalisation, and its accuracies should be read as an optimistic within-distribution bound, with the subject-out numbers providing the conservative estimate.

\subsection{Evaluation Design}

We evaluated the four reconstruction pipelines with a three-tier design. The first tier measures sample-level fidelity, the second tier measures trend-level similarity, and the third tier measures downstream discriminative utility. This structure is important because the three questions are not equivalent: a method can have large pointwise errors but still preserve enough temporal structure for a classifier, or it can reconstruct amplitudes accurately while losing information that matters for a specific task. Prior work on regression error metrics and time-series similarity supports using complementary measures when each metric is tied to a specific interpretation \cite{Botchkarev_2019,Ding2008}.

In the first tier, MAE, MRE, MSE, and Pearson correlation are used to compare the original and reconstructed samples directly. MAE gives an interpretable average absolute deviation, MSE penalizes large local failures, MRE normalizes error by signal magnitude, and Pearson correlation measures whether the temporal pattern is linearly aligned even when amplitudes differ. No single metric is sufficient, so these values are interpreted together rather than collapsed into one score.

In the second tier, each min--max normalized window is summarized by a global cubic polynomial. The coefficient vector is \((a_3,a_2,a_1,a_0)\) in the standard power basis, not a piecewise cubic-spline representation. This choice intentionally discards local sample-level detail and tests whether the reconstructed signal preserves broad trend shape. Euclidean and Manhattan distances measure coefficient magnitude differences, cosine similarity measures directional agreement, and Mahalanobis distance accounts for the covariance structure of the original coefficient vectors \cite{Shirkhorshidi2015}.

In the third tier, we measure original-trained classifier robustness to reconstructed inputs. Each classifier is trained once on the original triaxial windows of the training split and then evaluated, without retraining, on the original and reconstructed versions of the same held-out windows. Classification is included because reconstructed signals should retain task-relevant information, but this experiment measures distribution shift relative to a raw-signal classifier; it is not a direct augmentation experiment. Table~\ref{tab:classification_scenarios} separates the scenario reported in this paper from the additional train/test regimes that would be needed to claim augmentation utility. The per-window encoder metadata reused by the decoders (per-axis min--max, the MTF quantile interval, the sign conventions, and $\alpha$) is part of this controlled inverse problem and must not be interpreted as an augmentation setting, where such metadata would be unavailable.

\subsubsection{Statistical Significance Protocol}
\label{sec:statistical_protocol}
Every comparison reported in this paper is paired. For each LOTO fold, the four reconstructions and the original signal are evaluated on the same $\sim$2\,724 test windows, and each classifier is fit once per fold and reused across the five sources within that fold. Per-fold paired correctness vectors are then concatenated across folds 0, 1 and 2, yielding a pooled paired sample of $N=8\,172$ windows that drives all the inferential tests. Pooling is statistically valid here because each window is a single paired observation -- it never appears both in training and in test within the same fold, and the test set of a fold never contains a window from any other fold's test set. One caveat remains: because each fold is scored by its own separately-trained model, the pooled $2\times 2$ table aggregates discordant pairs produced under three different decision rules, so the homogeneity assumption behind a single McNemar table holds only approximately and the resulting $p$-values should be read as mildly anti-conservative. A stratified Cochran--Mantel--Haenszel or per-fold McNemar would be the more conservative alternative; since all but a handful of the reported comparisons reach $p_{\text{Holm}}<10^{-9}$, this caveat materially affects only the borderline cases we flag as such (Original-vs-GADF on KNN, $p_{\text{Holm}}=1.6\times10^{-2}$, and the RP-vs-MTF gaps that are no longer significant once the tuned $\rho=0.60$ lifts RP, such as SVM under LOTO, $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.52$). A second concern is that the pooled window sample is not fully independent --- many test windows share a subject, so window-level $p$-values could in principle be inflated by intra-subject pseudoreplication. We therefore repeat the pointwise comparison at the subject level, collapsing each subject to a single mean-MAE value so that the $N=51$ subjects, rather than the $8\,172$ windows, are the independent units (reported in Section~\ref{sec:disc_pointwise}).

For \emph{accuracy reporting}, every accuracy cell in the per-classifier tables (Tables~\ref{tab:gru_classifiers}, \ref{tab:knn_classifiers}, \ref{tab:cnn_classifiers}, \ref{tab:rf_classifiers}, \ref{tab:svm_classifiers}, and~\ref{tab:lstm_classifiers}) is the mean of the three fold accuracies and the dispersion is reported as the standard deviation across folds. Fold-level dispersion is a more honest measure of generalisation than a within-fold bootstrap, which only captures sampling noise inside a single split.

For \emph{paired classifier comparisons} we use the continuity-corrected McNemar test on the pooled discordant pairs \cite{Dietterich1998} -- the standard test when two classifiers are evaluated on the same instances -- and we distinguish two test families:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{F\textsubscript{M1}: Original vs.\ reconstruction.} For every one of the 4 reconstructions (RP, MTF, GASF, GADF) and every one of the 6 classifiers we test whether the paired correctness vector differs from the original-signal baseline. This produces $4\times 6=24$ paired tests. These McNemar tests are degradation/difference tests, not formal equivalence tests; absence of significance is therefore reported as ``no detected degradation'' rather than proof of equivalence.
\item \textbf{F\textsubscript{M2}: encoder vs.\ encoder at fixed classifier.} For every pair of reconstructions and every classifier we ask which encoder yields the higher accuracy under that classifier. There are $\binom{4}{2}=6$ encoder pairs and 6 classifiers, hence $6\times 6=36$ paired tests.
\end{itemize}

For \emph{paired pointwise-error comparisons} between encoders we use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test on per-window MAE \cite{Wilcoxon1945}. This test compares two reconstructions on the same windows and does not involve a classifier, so the family contains only $\binom{4}{2}=6$ tests (F\textsubscript{W}). Alongside each Wilcoxon $p$-value we report a non-parametric effect size $r=Z/\sqrt{N}$, where $|r|\geq 0.5$ is conventionally read as a large effect \cite{Rosenthal1991}. For the specific claim that \emph{GASF and GADF differ in how strongly they correlate with the original}, the two Pearson correlations are dependent because they share the same vector of originals; we therefore use Steiger's z for dependent correlations \cite{Steiger1980}.

Because the three families F\textsubscript{M1}, F\textsubscript{M2} and F\textsubscript{W} contain $24$, $36$ and $6$ simultaneous tests respectively, raw $p$-values within each family are corrected with the Holm--Bonferroni step-down procedure \cite{Holm1979} at a family-wise error rate $\alpha=0.05$. Throughout the paper we report the Holm-adjusted value $p_{\text{Holm}}$ and call a comparison significant only when $p_{\text{Holm}}<\alpha$ inside its own family; the unadjusted $p$-values are not used to declare significance. This controls the family-wise error rate \emph{within} each family at $0.05$, not a single experiment-wide rate across all three families: treating the families as independent, the across-family error is approximately $1-(1-0.05)^3\approx 0.14$, so a comparison that is only marginally significant inside its family should not be read as protected at the $0.05$ level once the three families are considered jointly. We keep per-family control deliberately, because the families answer distinct questions (degradation relative to the original, encoder-vs-encoder ranking, and pointwise ranking) and are reported and interpreted separately.

\subsubsection{Pointwise Error Metrics}

To evaluate the reconstruction methods, we use the following error metrics:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mean Absolute Error (MAE):} This metric represents the average of the absolute differences between the original and reconstructed values. It provides a direct measure of the magnitude of the errors without considering their direction.

  \item \textbf{Mean Relative Error (MRE):} Calculated as
  \[
  \operatorname{MRE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{|x_i-\hat{x}_i|}{|x_i|+\varepsilon},
  \]
  where \(\varepsilon=10^{-8}\) prevents division by zero when accelerometer values are zero or close to zero. This metric normalizes the absolute error by the magnitude of the original signal.

  \item \textbf{Mean Squared Error (MSE):} By squaring each error before averaging, the MSE gives a higher penalty to larger errors, thereby emphasizing large deviations in the reconstruction.

  \item \textbf{Pearson Correlation Coefficient:} This statistical measure indicates the degree of linear correlation between the original and reconstructed signals. A coefficient close to 1 indicates that the reconstructed signal follows the original temporal pattern.
\end{itemize}

These metrics are computed for each accelerometer axis (Dim 0, Dim 1, and Dim 2) and then aggregated as a global mean, allowing axis-level errors and overall reconstruction quality to be reported separately for RP, MTF, GASF, and GADF.


\subsubsection{Polynomial Trend Metrics}
\label{sec:polynomial_trend_metrics}

To evaluate the performance of the polynomial representation reconstruction, the following normalized metrics were computed on the cubic coefficient vectors. Each coefficient dimension was standardized using the mean and standard deviation estimated from the original windows before distances were calculated. For Mahalanobis distance, the covariance matrix was estimated from the standardized coefficient vectors of the original windows and regularized as \(\Sigma_{\lambda}=\Sigma+10^{-6}I\) before inversion.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mean Euclidean Distance:}
    This metric calculates the average Euclidean distance between the standardized cubic coefficient vectors of the original and reconstructed signals. A lower value indicates a more accurate reconstruction.

    \item \textbf{Mean Manhattan Distance:}
    This metric represents the mean absolute difference (L1 norm) between the standardized cubic coefficient vectors, and it is sensitive to the overall magnitude of the differences.

    \item \textbf{Mean Cosine Similarity:}
    This metric evaluates the cosine of the angle between the standardized coefficient vectors, yielding a value close to 1 when the reconstructed polynomial follows the same direction as the original, regardless of magnitude differences.

    \item \textbf{Mean Mahalanobis Distance:}
    This metric measures the distance between the standardized coefficient vectors using \(\Sigma_{\lambda}^{-1}\), taking into account the variance and correlation structure estimated from the original coefficient vectors. It provides a multidimensional assessment of the similarity between the original and reconstructed polynomials.
\end{itemize}

The quantitative results for these metrics are presented in Table~\ref{tab:normalized_metrics} later in the document.
\subsubsection{Classifier Evaluation}

To test whether reconstructed windows retain task-relevant information, we evaluated them with several classifiers. These classifiers do not prove pointwise fidelity; instead, they indicate whether class-discriminative temporal structure remains available after reconstruction. The selection of classifiers was inspired by Ignatov et al. \cite{IGNATOV2018915}, who analyzed multiple classifiers on accelerometer-based activity recognition.

Specifically, we used a three-fold protocol with \(n=3\) partitions. Each fold holds out one partition for testing and uses the remaining two partitions for training, producing a 66.6\%/33.3\% train-test split per fold. The 8,172 instances correspond to triaxial windows, not to separate one-dimensional axes; therefore, each fold contains approximately 2,724 test windows. Reported metrics represent the average performance obtained across the three folds when comparing the original time series against their reconstructed counterparts.

A detailed overview of each classifier and its intended role is presented below:

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU):}
A recurrent neural network variant designed to effectively capture temporal dependencies in sequential data. Its gating mechanisms regulate the information flow, enabling recognition of complex patterns over time.

\item \textbf{K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN):}
An instance-based classifier that assigns labels based on the majority class of the closest $k$ neighbors. It acts as a baseline to determine how well separated the reconstructed classes are within the feature space.

\item \textbf{Convolutional Neural Network (CNN):}
A deep neural network architecture optimized for extracting local temporal patterns and spatial relationships through convolutional filters. CNN effectively identifies discriminative motifs critical for activity classification.

\item \textbf{Random Forest (RF):}
An ensemble classifier comprising multiple decision trees that independently vote to determine the final class prediction. RF robustly captures nonlinear relationships and interactions among features.

\item \textbf{Support Vector Machine (SVM):}
This classifier finds separating hyperplanes in high-dimensional feature spaces and tests whether reconstructed windows remain separable under a margin-based model.

\item \textbf{Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM):}
A recurrent neural network designed to model long-term dependencies in sequential data. Its gating mechanisms test whether longer temporal correlations remain available after reconstruction.

\end{itemize}

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
The results are presented from the most direct evidence to the most indirect: visual reconstruction, pointwise error tables, polynomial trend similarity, and downstream classification. This order makes clear when a method reconstructs the signal itself and when it merely preserves information useful for a classifier.

\subsection{Qualitative Reconstruction Analysis}
\subsubsection{Artificial Time Series}
For our first experiment, we construct a synthetic time series \( \mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 3} \) of length \(N=128\) by sampling an underlying parameter \(t\) uniformly over \([0,\pi]\).  Concretely, define
\[
t_i = \frac{i}{N-1}\,\pi,\qquad i=0,1,\dots,N-1,
\]
and let each vector \(\mathbf{w}_i = [w_{i,0},\,w_{i,1},\,w_{i,2}]^\top\) be given by
\[
w_{i,0} = \cos(t_i),
\qquad
w_{i,1} = \sin(t_i),
\qquad
w_{i,2} = \sin(t_i)\;-\;\cos(t_i).
\]
Thus the full data matrix is
\[
\mathbf{w} =
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos(t_0) & \sin(t_0) & \sin(t_0)-\cos(t_0) \\
\cos(t_1) & \sin(t_1) & \sin(t_1)-\cos(t_1) \\
\vdots    & \vdots    & \vdots             \\
\cos(t_{N-1}) & \sin(t_{N-1}) & \sin(t_{N-1})-\cos(t_{N-1})
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
This three-dimensional signal, composed of a pure cosine channel, a pure sine channel, and their difference, serves as a controlled sanity check for the RP, GASF, GADF, and MTF reconstructions. It is not intended to represent the full WISDM distribution; instead, it provides a visually interpretable case where phase, amplitude, and orientation errors are easy to detect. To keep the evidence separated, every figure in this subsection refers only to the artificial control signal. Figure~\ref{fig:artificial_encodings} shows the image encodings of the three artificial channels under each of the four techniques (RP is $127\times127$ because of the $N-1$ pair embedding; the others are $128\times128$).

A sensitivity analysis of the recurrence-rate parameter showed that the best RP reconstruction was not obtained at the initial setting ($\rho=0.75$), but in a broad and stable region around $\rho\approx0.60$ (Figure~\ref{fig:rp_rho_sensitivity}). We therefore fixed $\rho=0.60$ as the operating point for the RP decoder. Compared with $\rho=0.75$, this setting reduced the mean MAE from $2.9189$ to $2.8163$ (a $3.52\%$ relative improvement) and the median MAE from $2.4571$ to $2.1775$; the improvement held in $2051$ of $2724$ windows ($75.3\%$). A paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test confirmed that the MAE reduction is statistically significant ($p=7.79\times10^{-85}$, $Z=-19.52$, $r=0.374$). This sensitivity analysis is reported \emph{post hoc} to characterise decoder behaviour: $\rho=0.60$ was fixed as a representative operating point of the broad MAE plateau ($\rho\approx0.58$--$0.61$), not as a fold-specific optimum. The numerically best value on this split was $\rho\approx0.612$, so rounding to $0.60$ deliberately avoids over-fitting the analysed fold rather than selecting a per-fold argmin.

\subsection{Grouped Subject-Out Robustness}
\label{sec:loso_results}

The LOTO 3-fold protocol used so far holds out individual trials but allows windows from the same subject to appear in both the training and the test split of a fold. This is the standard configuration in the WISDM literature and the right test for whether a reconstruction preserves task-relevant temporal structure. It is not, however, a test of whether the model generalises to \emph{unseen subjects} -- the regime that matters most for wearable deployment. We therefore replicate the full evaluation under a grouped subject-out 3-fold protocol: the 51 WISDM subjects are partitioned by StratifiedGroupKFold into three disjoint groups of 16, 18 and 17 test subjects respectively, and the test set of one fold never shares a subject with either of its training folds. We use the term subject-out rather than strict leave-one-subject-out, since each fold holds out a disjoint group of subjects rather than a single subject. Encoding, reconstruction, and the six-classifier panel are exactly the same as for the LOTO protocol; only the train/test indices change. Each subject-out fold contains $\sim$2\,724 test windows so the pooled paired-sample size for inferential tests is again $N=8\,172$.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The encoder ranking is preserved under subject-out evaluation, including the RP/MTF lens inversion of Section~\ref{sec:disc_classification}.} This is the load-bearing robustness check for the structural argument: the LOTO orderings are not artefacts of allowing the model to memorise per-subject calibration. Under grouped subject-out validation the absolute accuracies drop by $0.06$--$0.14$ as expected (the model has never seen the test subjects), and GASF and GADF remain statistically tied and near-original on every individual classifier of every fold ($p_{\text{Holm}}=1.0$ for both Original-vs-GASF and Original-vs-GADF). The RP/MTF inversion, however, is protocol-sensitive: once the tuned $\rho=0.60$ lifts RP, MTF significantly outranks RP under subject-out only on Random Forest and LSTM ($p_{\text{Holm}}=8.4\!\times\!10^{-19}$ and $2.8\!\times\!10^{-9}$), while GRU, CNN and SVM become statistical ties and KNN reverses in RP's favour ($p_{\text{Holm}}=3.3\!\times\!10^{-70}$). The strong LOTO inversion therefore softens under the harder subject-out split, which is itself informative: the RP/MTF gap is the least stable feature of the ranking, whereas the near-original GASF/GADF top tier is not a within-trial artefact.
\item \textbf{GASF shows no detected degradation from the original under this paired test.} The paired McNemar test on the pooled subject-out sample fails to reject Original-vs-GASF in all six classifiers ($p_{\text{Holm}}=1.00$ for all six); the absolute subject-out accuracy drop of GASF relative to the original is bounded by $|\Delta\text{acc}|\leq 0.001$ across the six classifiers, identical to the LOTO behaviour. This replicates the near-equivalence pattern of Section~\ref{sec:disc_classification}, while still stopping short of a formal equivalence or information-preservation claim.
\item \textbf{Under subject-out validation, RP is the only reconstruction that strictly improves on the original, and only for KNN.} On KNN, RP reaches $0.549\pm0.029$ while the original signal only reaches $0.504\pm0.035$ ($+0.044$ acc; paired McNemar $p_{\text{Holm}}=5.2\!\times\!10^{-28}$ in RP's favour); GASF and GADF, being near-original reconstructions, simply track the raw signal ($0.504$ and $0.505$, $p_{\text{Holm}}=1.0$ against the original). The RP gain is driven by the tuned recurrence rate $\rho=0.60$ and also holds under LOTO ($0.686$ vs $0.642$). This is consistent with a regularization or low-pass hypothesis: the same smoothing that costs RP accuracy on the high-ceiling classifiers becomes beneficial for the instance-based KNN classifier under the harder subject-out split. However, a dedicated frequency-domain analysis -- e.g., original vs.\ reconstructed power spectral density, high-frequency energy ratios, and smoothed-original baselines such as moving average, Savitzky--Golay or Butterworth filters -- is required before interpreting RP as a denoising mechanism.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{GADF Scaling Ablation: $\alpha$ and Anchor Reliability}
\label{sec:gadf_ablation}

The GADF decoder has one free design parameter, the amplitude restriction $\alpha$, and one heuristic step, the boundary-row anchor. We ablate both directly. For each $\alpha$ we encode every axis of $1\,500$ WISDM windows ($4\,500$ axis-series) at that scaling, decode from (i) the raw floating-point GADF matrix and (ii) the 8-bit PNG the pipeline actually stores, and report the pointwise MAE and Pearson of each, together with the fraction of windows whose boundary row is mis-identified (the assigned boundary amplitude differing from the window's true extreme). Three observations settle the $\alpha=0.7$ choice empirically. First, the floating-point inverse is numerically \emph{exact} on all evaluated WISDM windows for every tested $\alpha$, including $\alpha=0.72$ (MAE $<10^{-6}$): the closed-form reconstruction carries no algorithmic error on this data, so the entire GADF reconstruction error reported elsewhere in this paper is 8-bit PNG quantisation, not decoder failure. This numerical exactness is empirical and does not extend the theoretical guarantee: only $\alpha<1/\sqrt{2}$ retains a dataset-independent branch-uniqueness guarantee, and $\alpha=0.72$ merely happens not to produce an out-of-branch angular difference on these particular windows. Second, that quantisation error grows sharply as $\alpha$ shrinks --- from $0.0158$ at $\alpha=0.7$ to $1.58$ at $\alpha=0.5$ --- because the denormalisation scales the stored-image resolution by $(x_{\max}-x_{\min})/(2\alpha)$, so a narrower amplitude range is written into fewer effective intensity levels. This dominates the opposite effect one might expect from the steep $\arcsin$ derivative near $|G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ij}|=1$: that instability is confined to a handful of extreme near-boundary entries, whereas the improved use of the quantisation range at larger $\alpha$ sharpens the entire matrix. The largest admissible $\alpha$ therefore yields the sharpest image, and $\alpha=0.7$ is the largest value below the branch-uniqueness threshold $1/\sqrt{2}\approx0.7071$; $\alpha=0.72$ already violates it (and, tellingly, does \emph{not} improve the PNG MAE). Third, the boundary anchor never fails on WISDM: the anchor-misidentification rate is $0\%$ at every $\alpha$, because per-window min--max normalisation forces both amplitude extremes to be present, so the extreme-mean row is always a genuine boundary. This sweep and the $0\%$ anchor-failure rate answer the two robustness questions Section~\ref{sec:gadf_reconstruction_proposal} left open, and separate floating-point from PNG error as that section requested.

\subsection{MTF Decoder Ablation: Ordering and Metadata}
\label{sec:mtf_ablation}

The MTF decoder has two design choices we need to defend empirically: the bin-ordering algorithm (cosine-pivot heuristic vs Fiedler-vector spectral) and the bin count $Q$. We ablate both on the LOTO 3-fold protocol against the same six classifiers used in the rest of the paper.

\paragraph*{Decoder ablation (at fixed $Q=4$).}
We compare four decoder configurations on the same encoded MTF images (Table~\ref{tab:mtf_ablation}): the cosine-pivot ordering heuristic, Fiedler-vector ordering, Fiedler-vector ordering with stored bin-midpoint metadata, and Fiedler-vector ordering with both midpoint metadata and a sign-bit. \subsection{Visual Reconstruction Trade-offs}
\label{sec:disc_visual}

Figures~\ref{fig:rp_comparison_wisdm}--\ref{fig:mtf_comparison_global} show the four reconstructions overlaid on the original triaxial window. \subsection{Reconstruction Error Analysis}
\label{sec:disc_pointwise}

The pointwise tables (Tables~\ref{tab:recurrence_plot}, \ref{tab:markov_fields}, \ref{tab:grammian_fields}, \ref{tab:gadf_fields}) confirm the visual ordering. GASF attains a global MAE of $0.0202$ and a Pearson correlation of $0.99988 \pm 0.00027$; the residual error is on the order of $1/255$, which matches the resolution of the 8-bit RGB intermediate and rules out a structural failure of the diagonal inverse. GADF reaches $\text{MAE}=0.0156 \pm 0.0196$ and Pearson $0.99999 \pm 0.00002$ -- marginally tighter than GASF itself and roughly two orders of magnitude below RP and MTF: once the $\arcsin$ branch, the boundary anchor and the sign convention are fixed, the closed-form inverse is exact up to the 8-bit PNG quantisation of the stored image, so its residual error sits on the same $1/255$ order as GASF's. RP yields $\text{MAE}=2.82 \pm 3.12$ and Pearson $0.728 \pm 0.200$; the standard deviations across windows are large because the sign-flip check works decisively on some windows and is borderline on others. MTF under the Fiedler-vector decoder of Section~\ref{sec:mtf_ablation} reaches $\text{MAE}=3.98 \pm 5.09$, $\text{MSE}=56.42 \pm 147.82$ and a Pearson correlation that averages to $0.003 \pm 0.835$. The wide $\pm 0.84$ spread is the diagnostic of the residual mirror ambiguity: per window, MTF correlates either strongly positively or strongly negatively with the original because the recovered amplitude order can land on the wrong side of the global flip, and the two outcomes cancel in the mean.

The four pointwise orderings are statistically significant under paired, distribution-free testing. Wilcoxon signed-rank on the per-window MAE pooled across the 3 LOTO folds ($N=8\,172$ paired windows) rejects equality between every pair of encoders ($p_{\text{Holm}}<10^{-200}$ for all six pairs). Four of the six effect sizes $|r|=Z/\sqrt{N}$ are in the large-effect regime under the Rosenthal convention ($|r|\approx 0.87$ for the four comparisons that pit a Gramian closed-form decoder against a heuristic one, GASF/GADF vs RP/MTF); the remaining two are medium effects -- RP-vs-MTF ($r=-0.474$), because the Fiedler MTF decoder closes most of the pointwise gap between RP and MTF that the cosine-pivot decoder had opened, and, tellingly, GASF-vs-GADF ($r=+0.457$), because the two Gramian variants are now so close that their per-window MAE gap has shrunk to a medium effect. The sign of $r$ recovers the ranking $\text{GASF}\approx\text{GADF}\succ\text{RP}\succ\text{MTF}$ on a per-window pointwise basis (GADF in fact marginally ahead of GASF), while the RP-vs-MTF gap is the one that -- as Section~\ref{sec:disc_classification} below shows -- inverts when the lens changes from pointwise error to downstream classification accuracy.

This window-level ordering is not an artefact of the large window count. Repeating the paired Wilcoxon at the subject level --- collapsing each subject to a single mean-MAE value so that the $N=51$ subjects are the independent units --- reproduces every sign with strong significance: per-subject mean MAE is $0.0148$ (GADF), $0.0199$ (GASF), $2.65$ (RP) and $3.71$ (MTF), and all six subject-clustered pairs reject equality ($p\leq7.4\times10^{-10}$), the GASF-vs-GADF pair among them in GADF's favour. The subject-clustered test therefore confirms $\text{GADF}\succ\text{GASF}\succ\text{RP}\succ\text{MTF}$ as a per-subject effect, not a pseudoreplication artefact of the $8\,172$ windows.

\subsection{Polynomial Trend Comparison}
\label{sec:disc_trend}

Compressing each min--max-normalised window into a global cubic-polynomial coefficient vector $(a_3,a_2,a_1,a_0)$ removes most sample-level noise and asks only whether the broad shape is right. The result (Table~\ref{tab:normalized_metrics}, computed with the standardized-coefficient distance metrics defined in Section~\ref{sec:polynomial_trend_metrics}) places the two Gramian decoders together at the top: GADF and GASF are effectively tied (cosine similarity $1.00000$ and $0.99994$; standardized Euclidean coefficient distance $0.00070$ and $0.00079$), followed by RP (cosine $0.92819$) and MTF ($0.90817$). Two observations matter: (i) RP's polynomial cosine similarity is much higher than its pointwise Pearson, which is consistent with our reading that RP captures shape but mis-scales amplitude, and (ii) MTF's polynomial cosine similarity is competitive with RP even though its pointwise correlation collapses to zero, supporting the view that MTF retains a usable rough trend even when it loses local amplitude -- a property that the Fiedler-vector spectral decoder of Section~\ref{sec:mtf_ablation} leverages explicitly into a classifier advantage (Section~\ref{sec:disc_classification}).

\subsection{Classification Performance}
\label{sec:disc_classification}

The six per-classifier tables preserve the GASF/GADF top tier on five of the six classifiers --- KNN being the exception, where RP leads --- and also reveal the RP/MTF inversion and where reconstructions are good enough to be downstream-equivalent.

GASF reconstructions show no statistically significant degradation relative to the originals across all six classifiers ($|\Delta\text{acc}|\leq 0.001$ on the 3-fold mean for KNN, SVM, LSTM, Random Forest, CNN, and GRU). The paired McNemar test \cite{Dietterich1998} confirms this on the pooled $N=8\,172$ windows: in every one of the six Original-vs-GASF comparisons the raw $p$-value lies between $0.45$ and $1.00$ and the Holm-adjusted value is $1.00$, so we do not detect a paired classification difference under this protocol (24-test family, $\alpha=0.05$). This is not a formal equivalence test: failing to reject a difference is not, by itself, proof of equivalence or information preservation. Combined with the near-zero pointwise error, it supports the narrower claim that GASF is empirically near-equivalent for the evaluated WISDM task under controlled encoding and stored min--max metadata. A formal equivalence claim would require a pre-specified margin, such as a TOST-style test or confidence interval on \(\Delta\)accuracy.

GADF shows no detected degradation relative to the originals on any classifier ($|\Delta\text{acc}|\leq 0.001$ on the 3-fold mean; on Random Forest GADF is even $0.001$ above the original). The paired McNemar test on the pooled $N=8\,172$ windows fails to reject Original-vs-GADF in all six classifiers ($p_{\text{Holm}}=1.0$ throughout), and equally fails to separate GADF from GASF ($p_{\text{Holm}}=1.0$ in all six GASF-vs-GADF pairs): no statistically detectable classification difference is observed between the two Gramian decoders or from the raw signal. The same McNemar family confirms that GADF significantly beats MTF on every classifier and beats RP on five of the six ($p_{\text{Holm}}<10^{-20}$); KNN is the exception, where RP significantly beats GADF ($p_{\text{Holm}}=7.3\!\times\!10^{-25}$, RP $0.686$ vs GADF $0.643$). So GADF sits with GASF at the top of the classifier ranking on five of the six classifiers, KNN being the one lens where the tuned-$\rho$ RP reconstruction leads. As with GASF, this is a difference test, not a formal equivalence test: absence of significance is reported as no detected degradation, not proof of equivalence.

RP loses $0.16$--$0.19$ accuracy on the high-ceiling classifiers and $0.11$ on SVM (3-fold means), but on KNN it \emph{improves} on the original by $0.044$ ($0.686$ vs $0.642$). The paired McNemar test rejects equality with the originals for all six classifiers ($p_{\text{Holm}}<10^{-25}$, dropping below $10^{-180}$ for SVM, Random Forest, LSTM, GRU and CNN); on KNN the rejection is in RP's \emph{favour} ($p_{\text{Holm}}=1.2\!\times\!10^{-25}$). With the tuned recurrence rate $\rho=0.60$ (Section~\ref{sec:rp_reconstruction_method}) the RP-KNN accuracy rises to $0.686$, above both GADF ($0.632$) and the original signal ($0.642$): RP-KNN, not GADF, is the reconstruction that most improves on the original under this classifier, and the gain persists under subject-out validation (Section~\ref{sec:loso_results}). Away from KNN, RP remains in the lossy regime on the high-ceiling classifiers, so its value is specific to the instance-based, geometry-sensitive setting.

MTF, under the Fiedler-vector decoder of Section~\ref{sec:mtf_ablation}, loses 0.11--0.16 accuracy on the high-ceiling classifiers, 0.10 on SVM, and 0.08 on KNN (3-fold means). Two findings about MTF deserve to be highlighted because they reverse the standard intuition that MTF is "good for classification, bad for reconstruction" only in a vague sense, and now make the claim quantitative and statistically signed.

\emph{First, MTF outranks RP on the high-ceiling classifiers.} The paired McNemar test on the pooled $N=8\,172$ sample rejects RP-vs-MTF equality in four classifiers, all in MTF's favour (Random Forest $p_{\text{Holm}}=3.5\!\times\!10^{-28}$, LSTM $3.7\!\times\!10^{-18}$, CNN $9.6\!\times\!10^{-8}$, GRU $5.0\!\times\!10^{-4}$); the SVM gap is no longer significant once $\rho=0.60$ lifts RP ($p_{\text{Holm}}=0.52$, mean accuracies $0.697$ vs $0.694$). KNN is the exception that reverses the trend strongly: $\text{RP}=0.686$ vs $\text{MTF}=0.559$, $p_{\text{Holm}}=8.5\!\times\!10^{-131}$ in RP's favour. We read this as a structural property of the two encodings: MTF discards within-bin amplitude but preserves quantile-ordered transition statistics, which is precisely the kind of representation a non-instance-based classifier needs; RP preserves amplitude (lower pointwise MAE, as Section~\ref{sec:disc_pointwise} shows) but the noise of the MDS-based reconstruction perturbs neighbourhood geometry, which is what KNN actually measures.

\emph{Second, the rank inversion between pointwise and classifier lenses is the central qualitative finding of this paper's MTF evaluation.} On the pointwise lens the ranking is $\{\text{GASF},\text{GADF}\}\succ\text{RP}\succ\text{MTF}$ (Section~\ref{sec:disc_pointwise}); on the classifier lens MTF significantly outranks RP on four classifiers (Random Forest, LSTM, CNN and GRU) --- giving $\{\text{GASF},\text{GADF}\}\succ\text{MTF}\succ\text{RP}$ there --- while SVM becomes a statistical tie after tuning ($\rho=0.60$) and KNN reverses strongly in RP's favour. The two rankings do not contradict each other -- they answer different questions -- and the fact that the inversion happens cleanly along the RP/MTF pair, rather than spreading across the ranking, supports a "classifier-oriented vs reconstruction-oriented representation" reading of MTF (transition statistics) versus RP (recurrence geometry). Per-class behaviour matches that reading: under MTF, classes for which the source signal is amplitude-monotonic (sitting, standing) survive the $Q=4$ quantisation reasonably well, and classes that depend on amplitude shape (jogging, stairs) lose precision but retain recall through the preserved transition order.

\subsection{Four-lens Convergence and the RP/MTF Inversion}
\label{sec:disc_convergence}

Aggregating across the four lenses we observe a partial convergence with one principled inversion. GASF and GADF sit together above RP and MTF for visual reconstruction, pointwise error, polynomial-trend similarity, and downstream classification --- on every lens except the KNN classifier, where the tuned-$\rho$ RP reconstruction leads. The two Gramian decoders show no statistically detectable classification difference from each other (McNemar $p_{\text{Holm}}=1.0$ in every GASF-vs-GADF pair) and are separated only by a medium effect on per-window MAE that marginally favours GADF (Sections~\ref{sec:disc_pointwise} and~\ref{sec:disc_classification}). The proximity between RP and GADF on directional pairwise structure -- discussed in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} -- is also visible in every lens: GADF is strictly antisymmetric, while signed RP is a zero-diagonal directional distance matrix whose external sign rule approximates a related pairwise-difference structure without guaranteeing exact antisymmetry.

The interesting feature is the RP/MTF pair, which inverts between lenses. On the pointwise lens RP is closer to the original than MTF ($\text{MAE}=2.82$ vs $3.98$; Wilcoxon $r=-0.474$); on the polynomial-trend lens RP is also ahead (cosine similarity $0.928$ vs $0.908$); but on the classifier lens MTF outranks RP on four of the six classifiers (paired McNemar $p_{\text{Holm}}<10^{-3}$), the SVM gap is no longer significant, and RP wins KNN. This is not a contradiction: the two encodings make different choices about what they discard. MTF discards within-bin amplitude but keeps quantile-ordered transition statistics, which is the right representation for non-instance-based classifiers; RP keeps amplitude (lower MAE) but the MDS-based reconstruction adds neighbourhood-geometry noise that hurts classifiers more than it hurts pointwise distance. The convergence at the top of the ranking and the inversion at the bottom together imply that, within the operating regime tested, the lenses are neither redundant nor universally consistent: they are sensitive to different aspects of the reconstruction, and the practical recommendation must therefore name the downstream task. For a reconstruction-faithful augmentation pipeline whose downstream consumer is a sample-level model, $\{\text{GASF},\text{GADF}\}\succ\text{RP}\succ\text{MTF}$ is the right ordering; for a pipeline whose downstream consumer is a non-instance-based classifier of activity classes, $\{\text{GASF},\text{GADF}\}\succ\text{MTF}\succ\text{RP}$ is.

\subsection{Scope of Claims}
\label{sec:disc_scope}

The empirical scope of the conclusions is the full LOTO 3-fold protocol and the grouped subject-out 3-fold protocol on the smartwatch accelerometer stream of WISDM, with windows of $N=128$ at 20\,Hz and five activities ($N=8\,172$ pooled paired test windows in each protocol). Within this scope, GASF and GADF are both near-original decoders, showing no statistically detectable classification difference from each other or from the raw signal, with GADF marginally ahead on pointwise fidelity; they form the top tier on every lens except the KNN classifier, where the tuned $\rho=0.60$ RP reconstruction improves on the original --- and on both Gramian decoders --- under both protocols. Per-fold accuracy standard deviations are bounded by $0.03$ under LOTO and by $0.05$ under grouped subject-out validation. The bottom of the ranking is lens-dependent: on the reconstruction lenses (pointwise and polynomial) the order completes to $\text{GASF}\succ\text{GADF}\succ\text{RP}\succ\text{MTF}$, while on the classifier lens MTF significantly outranks RP on four of the six classifiers, with SVM a statistical tie and KNN reversing in RP's favour, so the classifier ordering is $\text{GASF}\succ\text{GADF}\succ\text{MTF}\succ\text{RP}$ everywhere except KNN (Section~\ref{sec:disc_convergence}). GASF and GADF are therefore the most faithful pointwise decoders and empirically near-equivalent for the evaluated classification task, but we do not claim formal information preservation. GADF is a closed-form restricted-branch decoder that matches GASF on every lens once its branch, anchor and sign convention are fixed. The clearest case of a reconstruction improving on the raw signal is RP-KNN: with the tuned $\rho=0.60$ it exceeds the original under both LOTO and subject-out validation --- a result consistent with a regularization hypothesis but not sufficient to prove denoising. RP is otherwise an amplitude-preserving but classifier-fragile decoder, valuable specifically in the instance-based, geometry-sensitive KNN setting; and MTF, under the spectral decoder of Section~\ref{sec:mtf_ablation}, is a classifier-friendly but amplitude-coarse representative reconstruction. The practical recommendation must therefore name the downstream consumer rather than collapse the comparison into an unconditional verdict on any single encoding. The broader generative-augmentation claim is not addressed by these numbers: every decoder in this paper assumes that the image is the deterministic output of its encoder, with side information (min--max, quantisation interval, sign convention) stored at encoding time. A diffusion or GAN that synthesises an image without storing those values would have to either learn them implicitly or be paired with a calibration step before any of these decoders can produce a physically plausible signal.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
Several limitations bound the interpretation of these results and should be read alongside the rankings above.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{LOTO is a within-distribution split.} Its grouping key is effectively unique per window, so it measures robustness to reconstruction-induced distribution shift rather than generalisation to unseen subjects; the grouped subject-out (subject-disjoint, not strict leave-one-subject-out) numbers are the conservative estimate and the ones relevant to deployment.
  \item \textbf{Post-hoc $\rho$ selection.} The recurrence rate $\rho=0.60$ was chosen from a sensitivity analysis on LOTO fold-0 and fixed as a representative of a broad MAE plateau, not as a nested cross-validation hyperparameter; a mild optimistic bias on that fold cannot be excluded, although the improvement replicates across folds and under subject-out validation.
  \item \textbf{Metadata dependence is conditional on the output domain.} Each decoder recovers its \emph{normalised} reconstruction from the image plus its known encoder settings (the GADF/RP sign conventions and $\alpha$, the MTF quantile count); the stored per-axis min--max is needed \emph{only} to map that normalised series back to physical accelerometer units (Section~\ref{sec:shared_protocol}). The encoder settings, and physical-scale metadata when physical units are wanted, are unavailable for arbitrary generated images, which is why the work is framed as controlled reconstruction rather than generative augmentation.
  \item \textbf{GADF anchor and quantisation.} The angular boundary row is identified by an extreme-mean heuristic; the ablation of Section~\ref{sec:gadf_ablation} finds a $0\%$ anchor-misidentification rate across the swept $\alpha$ on WISDM, but this is dataset-dependent (near-constant windows have no reliable boundary), and 8-bit PNG storage remains the dominant error source at small $\alpha$.
  \item \textbf{Approximate McNemar homogeneity.} The pooled $2\times2$ table aggregates discordant pairs scored by three separately-trained per-fold models, so its $p$-values are mildly anti-conservative; borderline comparisons are flagged as such, and a per-fold or Cochran--Mantel--Haenszel test would be stricter.
  \item \textbf{Intra-subject dependence.} Test windows from the same subject are not independent, so the window-level $p<10^{-200}$ values overstate the effective sample size. The subject-clustered Wilcoxon ($N=51$ subjects) in Section~\ref{sec:disc_pointwise} confirms the pointwise ordering at $p\leq7.4\times10^{-10}$; an analogous subject-clustered or mixed-effects analysis of the classifier comparisons is left to future work.
  \item \textbf{No formal equivalence test.} GASF's ``no detected degradation'' is a failure to reject a difference, not a proof of equivalence; a TOST-style margin on $\Delta$accuracy would be required for the stronger claim.
  \item \textbf{Single dataset and protocol.} All numbers come from the WISDM smartwatch accelerometer stream, five activities, $N=128$ windows at 20\,Hz; the rankings need not transfer to other sensors, sampling rates, activity sets, or window lengths.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:reproducibility}
All experiments use the WISDM smartwatch accelerometer stream \cite{misc_wisdm_smartphone_and_smartwatch_activity_and_biometrics_dataset__507} segmented into non-overlapping $N=128$ windows at 20\,Hz ($8\,172$ triaxial windows). The pipeline runs on Python $\geq3.10,<3.12$ with TensorFlow 2.11--2.15 for the neural classifiers (CNN, GRU, LSTM) and scikit-learn for KNN, SVM and Random Forest. Two design choices keep the reconstruction reproducible: the RP decoder uses classical (Torgerson) MDS, which is a deterministic eigendecomposition rather than a randomly-initialised iterative solver, and the parallel reconstruction carries an explicit sample index so decoded windows stay aligned with their labels regardless of completion order. Both the LOTO and grouped subject-out 3-fold partitions are produced by a fixed \texttt{StratifiedGroupKFold} split. The encoders, the four decoders, the six-classifier panel, the statistical-test script, and the table-emission and verification scripts that regenerate every table in this paper from the stored results are provided in the accompanying code repository.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have presented a controlled, symmetric evaluation of four image-based encodings -- RP, GASF, GADF, MTF -- as inverse mappings from image to triaxial time series. Three of the four reconstruction pipelines used in this evaluation are themselves new operational contributions of this work and, to the best of our knowledge, have no prior fully specified counterpart in the literature reviewed here: the signed-RP-aware decoder of Section~\ref{sec:rp_reconstruction_method}, which uses the signed Lu--Tong RP definition as an in-loop constraint that resolves the multidimensional-scaling reflection ambiguity left open by Hirata, Horai, and Aihara \cite{hirata2008reproduction}; the restricted-branch, boundary-row-anchored GADF decoder of Section~\ref{sec:gadf_reconstruction_proposal}, which turns the previously stated but under-specified invertibility of GADF on restricted angular ranges into an explicit operational decoder with branch control, angular anchoring, fixed sign convention, min--max calibration, and empirical evaluation; and the Fiedler-vector spectral MTF decoder of Sections~\ref{sec:mtf_reconstruction_method} and \ref{sec:mtf_ablation}, which reconstructs a representative compatible quantile trajectory rather than a bijective inverse of MTF. The fourth pipeline, the GASF decoder, is the only one that has direct precedent in the literature \cite{huamin2020reconstruction} and is kept in the comparison as a tightly controlled prior-art baseline. The four encoders are exercised on the same dataset, the same window size ($N=128$), both the LOTO 3-fold protocol and a grouped subject-out 3-fold protocol ($N=8\,172$ pooled test windows in each), the same classifier panel, and the same set of pointwise, polynomial-trend, and classification metrics. A symmetric evaluation across all four encodings -- under two complete 3-fold cross-validations, one within-trial and one subject-out -- is what allows the comparison reported here to be read as a direct ranking rather than as a partial inventory.

In summary, the four encodings occupy a clear hierarchy under the controlled protocol:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{GASF} --- near-original on every lens and empirically near-equivalent to the raw signal for classification; one of the two near-original reconstruction baselines, tied with GADF.
  \item \textbf{GADF} --- near-original on every lens and statistically tied with GASF, with a fully specified closed-form decoder; marginally the tighter of the two on pointwise error.
  \item \textbf{RP} --- amplitude-preserving but classifier-fragile in general; with the tuned $\rho=0.60$ it is the one reconstruction that beats the raw signal under the instance-based KNN classifier (both protocols), and it remains the natural choice when recurrence geometry itself is the object of study.
  \item \textbf{MTF} --- weakest point-to-point, but its quantile-transition structure makes it competitive with, and often above, RP for the non-instance-based classifiers.
  \item \textbf{Scope} --- all decoders assume the image is the deterministic output of a known encoder with stored metadata; this is controlled reconstruction, not a demonstration of generative augmentation.
\end{itemize}

Within the tested protocol, GASF is the most faithful pointwise decoder, with a global MAE of $0.0202$, a Pearson correlation of $0.99988 \pm 0.00027$, and a classification accuracy that matches the originals within $0.001$ absolute accuracy across the six classifiers on the 3-fold mean. The paired McNemar tests detect no GASF degradation in the evaluated classifiers, but this should be read as empirical near-equivalence for this task rather than as formal information preservation. The restricted-branch, boundary-row-anchored GADF decoder matches GASF on the pointwise and polynomial reconstruction metrics and on classification (Pearson $0.99999 \pm 0.00002$, polynomial cosine similarity $1.00000$, and no detected classification difference from the originals or from GASF, $p_{\text{Holm}}=1.0$ throughout), confirming that the diagonal failure observed in the original GAF paper does not preclude reconstructability from the full matrix once an angular anchor, a sign convention, and a restricted branch are fixed (scale metadata being required only to return to physical units). The two Gramian variants are near-identical rather than one dominating the other: Steiger's z for dependent correlations \cite{Steiger1980} on the pooled 3-fold sample, with $(r_{\text{orig,GASF}}=0.99988,\;r_{\text{orig,GADF}}=0.99999,\;r_{\text{GASF,GADF}}=0.99995)$ over $N=8\,172$ windows, yields $z=-131.6$, $p<10^{-300}$; the residual gap is tiny and marginally favours GADF, so neither Gramian variant is meaningfully more faithful than the other in this protocol.

The bottom of the ranking is lens-dependent. RP is an amplitude-preserving decoder ($\text{MAE}=2.82\pm 3.12$, Pearson $0.728\pm 0.200$) whose sign-flip ambiguity is resolved by the signed-RP orientation check --- the decisive component of the decoder, worth a $43\%$ MAE reduction (Section~\ref{sec:rp_ablation}) --- while the $N{-}1$ adjacent-state shrinkage is compensated by a per-window mean re-augmentation whose exact fill value we find immaterial. MTF, under the Fiedler-vector decoder of Section~\ref{sec:mtf_ablation}, is a classifier-friendly but amplitude-coarse decoder ($\text{MAE}=3.98\pm 5.09$, Pearson $0.003\pm 0.84$) whose Q=4 quantisation is information-theoretically lower-bounded by the within-bin variance but whose transition structure is preserved exactly by the Markov representation. On the pointwise lens RP wins ($\text{Wilcoxon }r=-0.474$ in favor of RP-vs-MTF MAE), but on the classifier lens MTF wins on four of the six classifiers (paired McNemar $p_{\text{Holm}}<10^{-3}$ in Random Forest, LSTM, GRU and CNN; the SVM gap is not significant; KNN is the exception that goes RP$\succ$MTF). The two encodings therefore make different trade-offs about what they discard, and the practical recommendation must therefore name the downstream consumer rather than collapse the ranking into a single number.

\paragraph*{Operator-level comparison of pairwise structure.}
Before discussing the first-order amplitude expansion that the rest of this section relies on, it is useful to put signed RP and GADF in a common notation without claiming equality of their output spaces. Both can be written as pairwise functions of lifted scalar samples,
\[
M_{ij} \;=\; \omega\!\bigl(\xi(x_i),\,\xi(x_j)\bigr),
\]
where $\xi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}$ is a two-dimensional lift of the scalar sample and $\omega:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}$ is the pairwise operator associated with the encoding. The two methods differ in both the lift and the operator:
\begin{itemize}
  \item RP under the Lu--Tong convention uses the adjacent-state embedding \(\xi_{\text{RP}}(x_k)=(x_k,\,x_{k+1})\) and a signed Euclidean distance, \(\omega_{\text{RP}}(u,v)=\sigma(u,v)\lVert u-v\rVert_{2}\), where the sign is assigned \emph{externally} by the projection onto the reference direction \(v_{\star}=(1,1)^{\top}/\sqrt{2}\). With the thresholded sign rule used here, \(\sigma(u,v)\) is not guaranteed to satisfy \(\sigma(u,v)=-\sigma(v,u)\) for every pair, so the resulting matrix is directional but not necessarily antisymmetric.
  \item GADF uses the polar lift \(\xi_{\text{GADF}}(\tilde x_k)=(\tilde x_k,\sqrt{1-\tilde x_k^{2}})=(\cos\phi_k,\sin\phi_k)\) and the operator \(\omega_{\text{GADF}}(u,v)=\det[v\mid u]=u_{2}v_{1}-u_{1}v_{2}=\sin(\phi_i-\phi_j)\), the signed area of the parallelogram spanned by the two lifted points, whose sign is \emph{intrinsic} to the operator and matches \(G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ij}\) in Eq.~\eqref{eq:gadf_indexed}.
\end{itemize}
The important consequence is therefore an analogy, not an identity. GADF lives in the $\binom{N}{2}$-dimensional space of antisymmetric zero-diagonal matrices by construction, because the determinant changes sign when its arguments are swapped. Signed RP has a zero diagonal and carries directional pairwise-distance information, but the Lu--Tong sign rule used in this paper does not force exact antisymmetry for nearly orthogonal displacements. The first-order expansion below quantifies why the two images can nevertheless look and behave similarly, while the three concrete advantages of GADF over RP (intrinsic sign, closed-form $O(N)$ inverse, no recurrence-rate threshold) follow from the determinant-based operator rather than from an exact shared matrix space.

\paragraph*{A structural reading of why RP and GADF look (and behave) so similarly.}
Beyond the empirical ranking, the proximity between RP and GADF deserves a structural reading: their visual resemblance and their adjacent positions in the four-lens ranking are not coincidental. GADF produces an antisymmetric zero-diagonal matrix, while signed RP produces a zero-diagonal directional distance matrix with related pairwise-difference content. For smooth signals, both reduce, in first order, to functions of the same pairwise-difference matrix $\{x_i-x_j\}_{i,j}$. The signed RP under the Lu--Tong embedding satisfies
\[
R_{ij} \;\approx\; \sigma(i,j)\,\sqrt{2}\,|x_i-x_j|,
\]
where $\sigma(i,j)$ is the external orientation flag computed from the projection of $s_i-s_j$ onto $v=(1,1)^\top/\sqrt{2}$. The GADF entry, expanded around a common normalized amplitude $\bar{x}$, satisfies
\[
G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ij} \;=\; \sin(\phi_i-\phi_j) \;\approx\; \frac{\bar{x}_j-\bar{x}_i}{\sqrt{1-\bar{x}^2}},
\]
because $d(\arccos x)/dx=-1/\sqrt{1-x^2}$, with both the samples $\bar{x}_i,\bar{x}_j$ and the expansion point $\bar{x}$ taken in the restricted $[-\alpha,\alpha]$ normalization of the GADF decoder. The sign of the GADF term is intrinsic to the trigonometric form rather than imposed externally. Thus, both expressions are linear in pairwise amplitude differences at first order and both are zero on the diagonal, but only GADF is strictly antisymmetric under the stated construction. What makes the restricted-branch GADF decoder closed-form is that it (i) makes the sign part of the encoding rather than a post-hoc convention, and (ii) restricts the angular range to $[\arccos(\alpha),\arccos(-\alpha)]$ with $\alpha=0.7$, a near-maximal symmetric choice below $1/\sqrt{2}$ that keeps every $\sin(\phi_i-\phi_j)$ inside the principal branch of $\arcsin$. In other words, GADF and signed RP are structurally adjacent parametrizations of directional pairwise differences: GADF is the parametrization that makes the inverse map explicit under the branch restriction, RP is the one that preserves adjacent-state recurrence geometry but pays for it with the MDS sign-flip step and the threshold $\rho$. This reading clarifies why GADF is the natural decoder to ``rescue'' from the RP family --- they are closer to each other than to either GASF or MTF, and the restricted-branch GADF decoder sidesteps precisely the orientation ambiguity that limits RP.

\paragraph*{Practical implication: \{GASF, GADF\} suffices, RP becomes redundant for reconstruction.}
The structural analogy above has a direct consequence for protocol design. The visual resemblance between the RP and GADF images is not a coincidence of pixel statistics --- it follows from the first-order relations $R_{ij}\propto \sigma(i,j)|x_i-x_j|$ and $G^{\mathrm{D}}_{ij}\propto (\bar{x}_j-\bar{x}_i)/\sqrt{1-\bar{x}^2}$ derived above, and the null-corrected regional-overlap diagnostic of Section~\ref{sec:disc_rp_gadf_overlap} confirms that matched RP/GADF windows share more spatial region structure than same-activity random pairings. Because GADF captures a closely related directional pairwise-difference structure with three concrete advantages --- (i) intrinsic sign through $\sin$, (ii) a closed-form boundary-anchored inverse, and (iii) no dependence on the recurrence-rate threshold $\rho$ --- the pair $\{\text{GASF}, \text{GADF}\}$ already covers the most reconstruction-relevant content that RP contributes in the controlled setting of this paper. GASF encodes the symmetric, amplitude-bearing part of the Gramian (diagonal $\cos(2\phi_i)$); GADF encodes the antisymmetric, difference-bearing part ($\sin(\phi_i-\phi_j)$); RP encodes a directional distance structure with additional sensitivity to the local derivative through the $(x_i,x_{i+1})$ pair embedding. Our empirical numbers largely confirm this analytical picture: GADF outranks RP on the pointwise and polynomial reconstruction metrics and on five of the six classifiers, so for the controlled reconstruction problem studied here we recommend the $\{\text{GASF}, \text{GADF}\}$ pair as the canonical reconstruction baseline. The one exception is the instance-based KNN classifier, where the tuned $\rho=0.60$ RP reconstruction beats both GADF and the original signal (Section~\ref{sec:disc_classification}); RP therefore retains specific value for geometry-sensitive, instance-based downstream models and for dynamical-systems analysis (recurrence quantification, dynamical invariants, line-structure statistics), rather than as a general invertible-imaging baseline. This recommendation does not extend to use cases where the RP threshold and adjacent-state embedding are themselves the object of study; it is specific to the question of using image encodings as a reversible substrate for generative augmentation.

These results invite three follow-ups. First, the robustness of each decoder under perturbations of the encoded image -- additive noise, JPEG/PNG re-quantisation, partial occlusion -- is the natural test of the augmentation hypothesis and is the missing piece for a generative-augmentation claim. Second, the GADF angular anchor is currently identified heuristically as the extreme (boundary) row; a principled selection rule (or a learned anchor predictor), together with sweeps over \(\alpha\), anchor-error rates, image-noise levels, and true-vs-global-vs-predicted min--max metadata, could close part of the GADF--GASF gap without leaving the closed-form regime. Third, the regularizing effect we observe under KNN --- where the tuned $\rho=0.60$ RP reconstruction beats the original under both protocols and GADF does so under subject-out validation --- deserves a controlled study of its own. The result is consistent with a low-pass hypothesis, but power spectral density, high-frequency energy ratios, and smoothed-original baselines are required before interpreting these reconstructions as a denoising mechanism. We expect that fixing these three follow-ups in turn would clarify when reconstruction-faithful encodings can substitute for the raw signal in downstream pipelines and when they cannot.
